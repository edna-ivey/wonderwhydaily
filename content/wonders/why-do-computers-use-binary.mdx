Computers use binary because their hardware can handle two clear states very reliably.

Inside electronic circuits, it is easier to tell "low voltage" from "high voltage" than to measure many delicate levels perfectly. A little electrical noise might blur a signal, but if the choices are separated enough, the circuit can still decide whether it is reading a 0 or a 1.

Those 0s and 1s are called bits. One bit is simple. Many bits together can represent numbers, letters, colors, sounds, instructions, and entire programs. The power comes from patterns and scale.

Binary is not the only number system humans can imagine. Computers could be designed in other ways, and some experimental systems use more than two states. But binary became dominant because two-state electronics are simpler, sturdier, and easier to build at enormous speed.

That is the strange bargain at the heart of digital life: make each tiny choice almost boringly simple, then make billions of those choices incredibly fast. Suddenly you have a movie, a game, a song, or a message from someone across the planet.
