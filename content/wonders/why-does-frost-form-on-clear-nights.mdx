Everything emits infrared energy. After sunset, the ground, plants, and roofs continue radiating energy upward. On a clear, calm night, little cloud cover is present to absorb some of that infrared radiation and send part of it back down.

Exposed surfaces can therefore cool quickly, sometimes becoming colder than the air just above them. If a surface falls below the frost point and below freezing, water vapor from the air deposits as ice crystals. Frost can grow into delicate branching patterns as more molecules join.

Clouds often limit this cooling by acting like an infrared blanket. Wind also mixes cold surface air with warmer air above, making frost less likely. Sheltered low spots can become especially cold because dense cool air drains downhill and collects there.

Weather-station temperatures are usually measured above the ground in a protected shelter. Grass or a car roof may be several degrees colder, so frost can appear even when the reported air temperature never reached 32 degrees Fahrenheit.
