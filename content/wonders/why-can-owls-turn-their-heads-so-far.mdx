Owls can turn their heads about 270 degrees because their necks have many flexible joints and their blood vessels are arranged to keep circulation working through the extreme motion.

An owl has 14 cervical vertebrae, twice the human number. Different sections of its long neck can twist, bend, and roll together, spreading the total rotation across many joints. The bird's lightweight head also pivots on one main socket where our skull has two.

Circulation is the harder problem. A human twisting that far could stretch or pinch arteries dangerously. Imaging studies found that owl neck arteries pass through roomy openings and have flexible connections. Reservoir-like widened vessels near the head and alternate routes can help maintain blood flow while some passages change shape.

Why turn instead of glancing sideways? Owl eyes are long, tube-shaped structures held firmly in their sockets, so they cannot swivel much like human eyeballs. A mobile neck aims both eyes and ears toward prey while the owl's body remains still and concealed.

The head does not spin all the way around, and an owl never disconnects it. Many small anatomical differences work together, turning a limited gaze into an extraordinary field of view.
