White sunlight contains a broad range of visible wavelengths. When light enters glass at an angle, it changes speed and direction, a process called refraction. It bends again when it exits into air.

The refractive index of glass varies slightly with wavelength. Violet light is generally slowed and bent more than red light. Passing through two angled faces magnifies that small difference, fanning the wavelengths into a visible spectrum.

A natural rainbow uses a related process inside countless water droplets. Light refracts as it enters a drop, reflects inside, and refracts again as it leaves. Each color reaches an observer at a somewhat different angle.

The prism is not coloring the beam. Evidence comes from recombination: lenses or another prism can bring the separated wavelengths back together, producing white light. The rainbow was hidden in the combined beam all along.

Different glass recipes produce different amounts of dispersion, which is why optical designers carefully choose materials when building lenses, cameras, telescopes, and scientific instruments.
