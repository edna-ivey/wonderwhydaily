Inside a phone, a tiny accelerometer measures acceleration along several axes. Even while the phone sits still, gravity produces a steady reading that points downward. Software compares the components of that signal to estimate which edge is closest to the ground.

A gyroscope adds information about rotation. It can sense how quickly the phone is turning, which helps the system respond smoothly and distinguish a deliberate change from a small shake. Devices often combine both sensors because each has weaknesses on its own.

The measurements are noisy, and ordinary movement also creates acceleration. Software filters many readings over time, checks whether the new orientation is stable, and then tells an app whether the device is portrait or landscape. That short pause prevents the screen from flipping wildly with every bump.

When a phone lies almost flat, gravity does not strongly favor one side edge, so orientation can be ambiguous. Rotation lock simply tells the interface to ignore automatic changes even though the sensors continue detecting movement for games, fitness tracking, camera stabilization, and other features.
