Snow on the ground is a jumble of ice crystals with air between them. When a boot presses down, the crystals bend, fracture, and slide past one another. Those tiny movements create vibrations that travel through the snow and air as sound.

Temperature changes the contact between crystals. Near freezing, a microscopic film of liquid water can coat parts of the ice and lubricate the grains. Colder snow is drier and more brittle, so its crystals scrape, snap, and resist sliding. Many tiny sounds combine into the familiar crunch or squeak.

The exact sound also depends on crystal shape, packing, age, and how quickly pressure is applied. Fresh powder sounds different from hard-packed snow, and boots spread force differently from skis or paws.

Snow does not announce a precise temperature, but a sharp squeak is a good clue that conditions are well below freezing. The ground beneath is not the instrument. The snowpack itself is responding to each compressed step.
