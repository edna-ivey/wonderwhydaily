Some planets have many moons because their size, location, and formation history gave them more material to build, capture, and hold in orbit.

Jupiter and Saturn grew enormous while the solar system was forming. Each was surrounded by a disk of gas and dust, almost like a miniature version of the disk around the young Sun. Material in those disks clumped into large regular moons. Their strong gravity later captured or rearranged smaller objects too.

Farther from the Sun, more ice was available as solid building material, which helped the outer planets and their moon systems grow. The giant planets also command large gravitational neighborhoods where moons can orbit without being pulled away by the Sun.

Earth's one large Moon probably formed after a giant impact blasted material into orbit around the young planet. Mars has two small moons, while Mercury and Venus have none. Moons can also collide, break apart, fall into a planet, or escape, so today's count is only the current chapter of a long history.

There was never a rule assigning one moon to each world. Every planet assembled in a different place and survived a different sequence of disks, impacts, captures, and losses.
