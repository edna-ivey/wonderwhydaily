Seals are mammals, so they must breathe air at the surface. Before diving, they load oxygen into the lungs, blood, and muscles. Their blood volume and oxygen-carrying hemoglobin are high, and their muscles contain abundant myoglobin, a protein that stores oxygen locally.

Once submerged, the dive response helps ration that supply. Heart rate slows, and blood flow is reduced to tissues that can temporarily tolerate less oxygen. The brain and heart keep receiving priority. Muscles can use their own myoglobin reserves.

Some diving seals exhale before descending, allowing flexible lungs to compress and reducing nitrogen absorption at depth. Their bodies also tolerate higher carbon dioxide and lower oxygen levels than humans comfortably can. Different species have very different dive limits.

The seal is not breathing water, and it is not holding a giant lungful alone. Its entire circulatory and muscular system acts as a managed oxygen tank, spending a limited resource where it matters most until the animal surfaces.
