A one-dimensional barcode represents digits with alternating bars and spaces of carefully chosen widths. A scanner shines light across the symbol. White spaces reflect more light and dark bars reflect less, creating a changing electrical signal.

Special guard patterns mark the beginning, middle, and end. The quiet blank zones outside the symbol help the scanner find its boundaries. In a UPC, the left and right halves also use related but different encoding patterns. These rules tell the decoder which direction it crossed the code.

If the scanner moves from the opposite side, the signal arrives reversed, but the recognizable structure lets software reorient it. Check digits provide an additional mathematical test that the decoded number is internally consistent.

A checkout scanner often sends beams in many directions so at least one crosses all the bars. The code does not need to be perfectly upright. It only needs a clear path across the full pattern with enough contrast for the reader to distinguish widths.
