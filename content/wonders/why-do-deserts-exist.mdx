Deserts exist where long-term patterns of air and geography deliver very little rain or snow, not simply where the ground becomes hot.

Near about 30 degrees north and south latitude, large-scale atmospheric circulation often sends dry, sinking air toward the surface. Sinking air warms and becomes less likely to form clouds. That helps create broad desert belts that include the Sahara and parts of Australia.

Mountains can make deserts too. Moist air forced up a mountain cools and drops rain or snow on the windward side. The air then descends on the other side warmer and drier, creating a rain shadow. Cold ocean currents can stabilize coastal air and limit rain, while places deep inside continents may sit far from ocean moisture.

Deserts can therefore be hot, cool, or bitterly cold. What unites them is low precipitation compared with the amount of moisture that could evaporate. Antarctica's icy interior qualifies even though sand dunes and scorching temperatures are absent.

A desert is not land that somehow used up its water. It is a region positioned where the planet's moving air and landscape repeatedly make moisture difficult to deliver.
