The winter solstice is the day with the shortest interval between sunrise and sunset, but clock times are controlled by more than total daylight. The moment when the Sun reaches its daily highest point, solar noon, shifts relative to our evenly ticking clocks.

Earth's orbit is slightly elliptical, so the planet moves faster near the Sun and slower farther away. Earth's axis is also tilted relative to its orbit. Together these effects make apparent solar time run a little ahead or behind mean clock time through the year, a pattern called the equation of time.

Before the solstice in many mid-northern locations, solar noon is shifting later quickly enough that sunset begins moving later even while the daylight interval is still shrinking. Sunrise continues getting later until after the solstice. The two changes balance on different dates.

Latitude and time-zone position affect the exact clock times, but the mismatch is normal. The shortest day describes duration; earliest sunset describes one endpoint on a human clock.
