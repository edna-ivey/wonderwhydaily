Temperature describes the motion and energy of particles. The vacuum of space contains so few particles that it cannot warm or cool an object through ordinary contact the way air or water does. There is almost no conduction or convection.

The Sun sends energy outward as electromagnetic radiation. A surface facing sunlight absorbs some of that energy and can become very hot. A surface in shade receives much less and continues radiating its own energy away, so it can become extremely cold.

Distance, color, reflectivity, orientation, and internal equipment all affect the balance. Spacecraft use reflective blankets, radiators, heaters, insulation, and careful rotation to keep components within working temperatures.

Calling space cold is useful shorthand, but it can hide the real physics. Vacuum does not blow cold onto an astronaut. It removes familiar pathways for heat transfer, creating sharp contrasts determined mostly by what radiation an object absorbs and emits.
