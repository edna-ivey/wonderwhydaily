Far from shore, a wind-made wave carries energy through deep water. Individual water particles mostly move in looping paths as the pattern travels onward. If the water is deep enough, those loops do not interact much with the bottom and the swell can remain low and smooth.

Near a beach, the depth shrinks. The lower part of the wave begins to feel the seafloor and slows because of friction and the changing water depth. The waves bunch closer together, while energy forces the crest higher. The front becomes steeper and steeper.

Eventually the crest is moving too fast and stands too high over its base. Gravity pulls it forward and it collapses, releasing energy as foam, sound, turbulence, and currents. Beach slope and wave shape influence whether the breaker spills gently, curls into a plunging tube, or surges up a steep shore.

Tsunamis behave differently because their wavelengths are enormous and the whole water column moves. They often reach land more like a rapidly rising flood than the familiar curling breakers made by shorter wind waves.
