Some beaches are black because dark rock or mineral grains collect along the shore.

Many black beaches sit near volcanic landscapes. When lava cools, it can form dark rocks such as basalt or volcanic glass. Waves pound cliffs and chunks of lava, while wind, rain, and tumbling stones help break the material into smaller and smaller pieces. Eventually, dark grains collect along the coast.

Fresh lava entering the ocean can also shatter as intensely hot rock meets cold water. The pieces are then sorted and reshaped by waves. Some grains remain rough and glassy; others become smoother after years of movement.

Not every black sand is exactly the same. Some black sand comes from heavy minerals such as magnetite that waves concentrate because those grains resist being carried away as easily as lighter sand.

White tropical sand often comes largely from shells and coral. Tan sand may contain quartz and other light minerals. Black sand is a dramatic reminder that every beach is borrowed geology. The shoreline is simply where waves pause long enough to display the pieces.
