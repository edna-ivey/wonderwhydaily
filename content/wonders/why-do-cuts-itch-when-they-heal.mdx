A healing cut can itch because the repair work can irritate nearby nerves.

A wound changes from moment to moment. Early inflammation brings cells and chemical signals to protect the area. Later, new tissue grows, the wound edges pull closer, and the surface may become dry beneath a scab or bandage. Any of those changes can make nearby nerve endings send itchy signals.

There is no single "healing itch" switch. Chemicals involved in inflammation can trigger itch. Dryness and tightening skin can add strange sensations. Nerves disturbed by the injury may also send mixed messages while the area recovers. The brain can interpret that combination as itch instead of pain, pressure, or tingling.

Scratching may feel satisfying briefly because stronger touch signals compete with the itch. Unfortunately, scratching can also reopen delicate tissue or introduce germs. Increasing redness, warmth, swelling, pain, drainage, or a severe spreading itch can signal that something else is going on and may need medical attention.

An itch can be part of healing, but it is only one message from a busy repair site, not a guaranteed all-clear signal.
