Bread dough contains amino acids from proteins and several kinds of sugars. During baking, heat lets these ingredients react through a network called the Maillard reaction. Hundreds of flavorful and aromatic compounds can form, along with larger brown molecules called melanoidins.

The reaction thrives on the loaf's surface because water evaporates there. Once the crust dries, its temperature can rise well above the boiling point of water. The moist crumb inside remains cooler for much of baking, limiting browning and keeping it pale and soft.

Caramelization of sugars can contribute at higher temperatures, but it is not the entire story. The Maillard reaction is especially important because it joins sugar chemistry with amino acids. Recipe, acidity, oven temperature, and baking time all alter the result.

That is why a golden crust tastes different from the center even though both began as the same dough. The oven creates two environments in one loaf: a steamy interior and a dry surface where heat builds a new set of flavors.
