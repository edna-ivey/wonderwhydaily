White table sugar is mostly sucrose. When dry sugar gets hot enough, its crystals melt and the molecules begin to react. Bonds break, water is released, and fragments rearrange into a large collection of new compounds. This process is caramelization.

Some products provide nutty, buttery, fruity, or toasted aromas. Others join into larger molecules that absorb visible light and give caramel its golden-to-deep-brown color. Temperature and time determine which mixture develops. Too little heat leaves plain sweetness; too much produces bitterness and eventually black carbon.

Caramelization is often confused with the Maillard reaction. Both create brown color and complex flavor, but Maillard browning involves reactions between sugars and amino acids, the building blocks of proteins. Bread crust and seared foods rely heavily on that second pathway.

Hot caramel can cause serious burns because it reaches temperatures far above boiling water and sticks to skin. The kitchen transformation is fascinating, but it belongs under close adult supervision.
