Bread rises because dough becomes a place for tiny living yeast cells to work.

Yeast feeds on sugars in the dough and releases carbon dioxide gas. If that gas simply escaped, you would not get much of a loaf. The trick is that wheat dough can form gluten, a stretchy network of proteins that traps the gas in little pockets.

As those pockets fill, the dough expands. Kneading, folding, and resting all help develop that elastic structure, which is why bread dough can feel alive even before it goes into the oven. It is not thinking, thankfully. It is just full of bubbles and chemistry.

When the dough hits the heat of the oven, the gas expands more, and the loaf gets a final burst of lift called oven spring. Then the yeast stops working, the starches and proteins set, and the soft bubble structure becomes bread you can slice.

Every hole in a piece of bread is a bubble that survived long enough to become part of breakfast.
