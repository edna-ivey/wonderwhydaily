Compasses can seem to point the wrong way because magnetic north and geographic, or true, north are different directions in most places.

True north points toward the geographic North Pole, the end of Earth's rotation axis. A compass needle instead lines up with the horizontal part of Earth's local magnetic field. That field is generated mainly by moving, electrically conducting metal deep in the planet's outer core, and its pattern is not a tidy set of circles around the globe.

The angle between compass north and true north is called magnetic declination. In one location the needle may point several degrees east of true north; elsewhere it may point west. Near a line called the agonic line, the two directions match. Navigators use maps or models to add the proper correction.

Declination also changes slowly because Earth's magnetic field changes. NOAA updates the World Magnetic Model so ships, aircraft, phones, and other navigation systems can account for those shifts. Nearby metal, electric currents, and magnetic rocks can create smaller local errors too.

The compass is not broken. It is faithfully answering a different question: not 'Where is the top of the globe?' but 'Which way does the magnetic field run right here, right now?'
