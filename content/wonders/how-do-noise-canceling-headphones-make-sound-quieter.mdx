Sound reaches your ears as repeating changes in air pressure. Active noise-canceling headphones use microphones to measure unwanted sound near or inside the ear cup. A processor quickly estimates the wave and tells the speakers to produce another wave with peaks where the noise has troughs and troughs where it has peaks.

When the two waves meet, they interfere. If their timing and strength line up well, their pressure changes partly cancel at your ear. The original sound has not been erased from the entire room. The headphones have created a small listening zone where much less of that pattern reaches you.

The method is especially good at reducing steady, low-frequency sounds such as an airplane engine, a fan, or a train's rumble. Sharp claps, nearby speech, and high notes change too quickly or have wavelengths that make exact cancellation more difficult. Padding still provides passive isolation by physically blocking some sound.

The electronics must keep measuring and adjusting because both you and the noise can move. That constant feedback is why active cancellation needs power and why a good pair can make a low mechanical hum seem to fade when you switch the feature on.
