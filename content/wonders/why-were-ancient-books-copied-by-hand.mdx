Ancient books were copied by hand because there was no practical machine that could quickly produce many identical pages.

A trained scribe read an existing text and reproduced it character by character on clay, papyrus, parchment, paper, or another writing surface. In some places scribes worked in royal offices, temples, monasteries, or commercial workshops. Copying preserved laws, accounts, stories, religious works, and scientific ideas, but it demanded time and skill.

Hand copying also created differences. A tired scribe might skip a line, spell a word differently, add a note, or deliberately correct what seemed like an error. Scholars today compare surviving manuscripts to understand how a text changed as generations copied it.

Printing methods existed in East Asia long before Gutenberg, including woodblock printing and movable type. In Europe, Gutenberg's mid-1400s system combined movable metal type, suitable ink, and a press into an efficient production method. Many nearly identical pages could then be printed from one arrangement of type.

Before that transformation, a book was not simply written once and duplicated. Every additional copy was another careful handmade object, and every surviving copy carried a small human history of its own.
