Most objects contain balanced amounts of positive and negative charge. When certain materials touch and separate, electrons can transfer from one surface to the other. Walking across carpet, removing a sweater, or sliding across a car seat can leave your body with an excess or shortage of electrons.

The charge may remain in place because air and many fabrics are poor conductors. Then your finger approaches a metal doorknob, which offers an easier route for charge to move. If the electrical difference is strong enough, electrons cross the tiny air gap before contact. The heated air glows briefly, producing a spark and a sharp snap.

Dry air makes static effects more noticeable because a thin coating of moisture normally helps charge leak away. In winter, indoor air often has less water vapor, so the imbalance can grow larger before it discharges.

The voltage can sound dramatic, but voltage alone does not tell the whole safety story. An everyday static spark contains very little charge and ends almost instantly. Static is still important around fuel vapors and sensitive electronics, where even a small spark or discharge can cause real damage.
