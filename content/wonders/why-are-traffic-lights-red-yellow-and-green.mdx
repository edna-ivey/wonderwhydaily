Long before electric traffic lights became common on roads, railways needed signals that engineers could recognize quickly from a distance. Red became the warning or stop color, while green eventually meant proceed. Yellow provided a caution signal between those two commands.

Road systems adopted the same basic language as automobiles multiplied. Using a small, standardized set of colors lets drivers learn one pattern and apply it at thousands of intersections. Red, yellow, and green are also separated enough in color and meaning to communicate distinct stages.

Color is not the only clue. In the United States, a vertical signal normally places red on top, yellow in the middle, and green at the bottom. That consistent position helps people who have difficulty distinguishing certain colors. Symbols, arrows, timing, and brightness add more information.

Modern signals may use sensors and computers, but their visible vocabulary remains simple. Red stops conflicting streams, yellow warns that the right of way is changing, and green permits movement when the intersection plan makes it safe.
