Fog appears when air near the ground becomes saturated and water vapor condenses into countless tiny droplets, then disappears when those droplets evaporate or move away.

Air can become saturated by cooling, by gaining moisture, or both. On a clear, calm night, the ground loses heat and chills nearby air until it reaches the dew point. Moist air can also move over a colder surface, or cold air can mix with warm vapor rising from water.

The droplets are individually tiny, but together they scatter light in many directions and hide distant objects. Fog is therefore not water vapor itself, which is invisible. It is a ground-level cloud made of liquid droplets, or sometimes ice crystals in very cold conditions.

After sunrise, warming ground can heat the lowest air and evaporate the droplets. Stronger wind may mix the fog with drier air, lift it into a low cloud, or move the whole bank elsewhere. A new air mass can clear fog rapidly, while stable cool and moist conditions can keep it for hours.

Fog seems to arrive from nowhere because the ingredients were already present but invisible. Crossing the saturation threshold turns vapor into visible droplets; crossing back makes the landscape reappear.
