Some frogs, especially wood frogs, survive freezing because their bodies pack tissues with protective chemicals and control where ice forms.

As temperatures drop, ice begins forming in spaces outside the frog's cells. Signals from that freezing trigger the liver to release large amounts of glucose, while urea also accumulates. These dissolved substances act as cryoprotectants, helping cells hold onto water and lowering the chance that sharp ice crystals form inside them.

Much of the frog's body water can freeze. Its breathing stops, and its heartbeat becomes undetectable. Yet enough liquid remains inside protected cells for their delicate structures to survive. The frozen animal is not carrying on normal life invisibly; its metabolism has slowed to an extraordinary minimum.

When spring warmth returns, ice melts, circulation restarts, and the frog gradually resumes breathing and movement. Timing matters because repeated freezes, extreme temperatures, or too much internal ice can still be fatal. Most frog species cannot tolerate this treatment at all.

The wood frog does not avoid winter by staying warm. It survives by managing a controlled freeze, turning ordinary sugar and waste chemicals into microscopic protection until the forest thaws.
