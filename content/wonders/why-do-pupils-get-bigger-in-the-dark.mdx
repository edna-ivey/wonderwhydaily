The pupil is the round opening at the center of the colored iris. Light passes through it on the way to the retina, the light-sensitive tissue at the back of the eye. Muscles in the iris automatically change the opening's diameter.

In bright conditions, the pupil constricts. A smaller opening limits incoming light and improves sharpness across a useful range of distances. In dim conditions, other iris muscles pull the opening wider so the retina receives more of the scarce light available.

This pupil reflex begins within seconds, but it is not the whole story of seeing at night. Retinal cells also change their chemistry and sensitivity over many minutes. That slower dark adaptation is why a room may reveal details gradually even after your pupils have already widened.

Pupil size also responds to focus, medicines, emotion, and signals from the nervous system. Doctors shine a light into the eyes because the speed and symmetry of the response can provide useful information about the eye and brain.
