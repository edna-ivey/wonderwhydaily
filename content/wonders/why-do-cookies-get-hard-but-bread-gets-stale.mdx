Cookies often become hard as they lose moisture to the air, while bread stales mainly because water shifts and its cooked starch molecules reorganize into firmer structures.

A fresh soft cookie contains water held among sugar, fat, flour, and other ingredients. In drier air, some of that water escapes. With less moisture keeping the cookie flexible, its sugar-and-starch structure feels harder. A crisp cookie can do the reverse in humid air and become soft as it absorbs water.

Bread has much more water and a springy network filled with gelatinized starch. After baking, starch chains slowly line up and form more ordered regions, a process called retrogradation. Water also migrates within the loaf. The crumb becomes firm and less pleasant even though the bread has not simply dried into toast.

That is why refrigeration often speeds bread staling, even though it slows mold. Freezing is better for longer storage because it greatly slows molecular movement. Gentle reheating can temporarily soften stale bread by disrupting some starch structures, but they form again as it cools.

Cookies and bread both respond to moving water, yet recipe and structure decide what we notice. One loses the softness water provided; the other rebuilds a firmer starch framework from within.
