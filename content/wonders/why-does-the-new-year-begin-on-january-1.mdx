A year is rooted in Earth's orbit, but its first day is a convention. Early Roman calendars changed over time and once began in March. January gained importance when Roman officials called consuls began taking office on January 1.

Julius Caesar's calendar reform took effect in 45 BCE and treated January 1 as the start of the civil year. The month was associated with Janus, a Roman god portrayed looking backward and forward, which gave the boundary symbolic force as well.

After the Roman Empire, European regions did not all agree. Some began the numbered year at Christmas, March 25, or Easter. The Gregorian calendar introduced under Pope Gregory XIII in 1582 corrected calendar drift and retained January 1, but countries adopted it at different times.

Today January 1 is a dominant international civil convention, not the only new year. Lunar, lunisolar, religious, and regional calendars begin their years on other dates. Calendars organize time by combining astronomy with culture and history.
