GPS satellites broadcast messages containing their position and the exact time each signal was sent. A receiver compares that timestamp with the arrival time. Because radio waves travel at the speed of light, travel time reveals the receiver's distance from the satellite.

Light covers about 300 meters in one microsecond. That means a clock error of only a millionth of a second can create a distance error roughly as long as three football fields. Satellites therefore carry atomic clocks, which use stable atomic frequencies to keep extraordinarily consistent time.

A phone's clock is not nearly as accurate. By receiving signals from at least four satellites, the GPS calculation can solve for latitude, longitude, altitude, and the phone clock's error together. More satellites and other sensors can improve the result.

Engineers must even account for relativity. Speed slows the orbiting clocks slightly, while weaker gravity speeds them up by a larger amount. Without correction, those effects would accumulate into serious navigation errors. Your map marker depends on physics, geometry, and exceptionally good timekeeping.
