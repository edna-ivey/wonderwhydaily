Old maps included sea monsters because the creatures could decorate valuable objects, visualize uncertain reports, symbolize danger, and fill parts of the ocean that mapmakers understood poorly.

Medieval and Renaissance Europeans inherited animal descriptions from classical writers, religious traditions, sailors, and travelers. Some reports began with real whales, walruses, giant squid, or unfamiliar fish, then changed through retelling and illustration. Other creatures were openly fantastic.

A monster could communicate that a region was remote or hazardous, but it was not a universal map symbol meaning 'unknown.' Many practical nautical charts contained few or no monsters. On luxury maps and globes, an elaborate animal showed the artist's skill and the patron's wealth. Buyers sometimes paid for extra decoration.

Images also traveled from map to map. Olaus Magnus's sixteenth-century Carta Marina displayed many northern sea creatures, and later mapmakers copied or adapted them. A picture could therefore persist long after its original observation or story was questioned.

These maps were not simply foolish science covered in mistakes. They combined navigation, geography, art, authority, commerce, and wonder. The monsters reveal what people had heard, what they feared, and how they pictured waters beyond familiar routes.
