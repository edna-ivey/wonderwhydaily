Batteries eventually die forever because their charge-and-discharge chemistry is never perfectly reversible, so unwanted reactions and structural damage accumulate over time.

A rechargeable battery moves ions through an electrolyte inside the cell while electrons travel through the outside circuit. Charging pushes the system toward a higher-energy chemical state; using the battery lets it move back while powering a device. Ideally, the same materials would cycle forever.

Real materials change. Side reactions consume mobile ions and electrolyte. Thin films can grow on electrode surfaces, increasing resistance. Electrode particles may crack, swell, lose contact, or develop new structures. Each cycle leaves a little less active material able to store and release energy.

A battery also ages while sitting still. High temperature and spending long periods extremely full or empty can speed some degrading reactions. Cold may temporarily reduce performance without causing the same permanent loss. Charging systems manage voltage and temperature to slow damage, but they cannot stop chemistry entirely.

When an old battery says 100 percent, it means 100 percent of its current reduced capacity, not its factory-new capacity. The meter can refill; the microscopic storage room has gradually shrunk.
