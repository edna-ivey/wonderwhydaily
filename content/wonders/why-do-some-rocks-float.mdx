Some rocks float because they contain so many gas-filled holes that the rock's average density is lower than the density of water.

The best-known example is pumice. It forms when gas-rich, sticky magma erupts and the sudden drop in pressure lets dissolved gases make bubbles, a little like foam appearing when a fizzy drink is opened. The molten rock cools quickly around that froth, trapping a maze of spaces inside.

A solid chunk of most rock is denser than water and sinks. Pumice includes both rock and air, so its total mass is spread across a larger volume. If enough pores remain filled with gas, the combined material can displace water weighing more than the pumice itself, and buoyancy holds it up.

Floating does not always last. Water can gradually enter connected pores, increasing the pumice's average density until it sinks. Different pieces may stay afloat for very different lengths of time because their bubbles vary in size, shape, and connection.

After some undersea eruptions, countless pieces gather into pumice rafts large enough to be tracked from satellites. A rock may feel like the definition of solid, yet a pumice stone is really frozen volcanic foam.
