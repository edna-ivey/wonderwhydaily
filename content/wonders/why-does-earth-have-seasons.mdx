Earth spins around an imaginary axis that is tilted about 23.4 degrees compared with the plane of its orbit. As the planet travels around the Sun, that axis keeps pointing roughly in the same direction. For part of the year, the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun; six months later, the Southern Hemisphere does.

A hemisphere tilted toward the Sun receives sunlight at a more direct angle, concentrating energy over a smaller area. It also stays in daylight longer each day. Those two effects create summer. When the same hemisphere tilts away, sunlight arrives at a shallower angle and the days shorten, producing winter.

Distance is not the main cause. Earth's orbit is slightly lopsided, but the planet is actually closest to the Sun in early January. At that moment the Northern Hemisphere is in winter while the Southern Hemisphere is in summer, which would make no sense if distance controlled the seasons.

Near the equinoxes, neither hemisphere tilts strongly toward the Sun and day and night are nearly equal. The solstices mark the greatest lean toward or away, giving each hemisphere its longest and shortest stretches of daylight.
