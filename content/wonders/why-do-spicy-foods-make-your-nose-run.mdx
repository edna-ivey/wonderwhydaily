Spicy foods make your nose run because spice-sensitive nerves trigger a reflex that tells nasal glands to make more mucus.

Chili peppers contain capsaicin. This molecule attaches to nerve receptors that normally help detect real heat and irritation. Your brain receives a message that sounds a lot like, "Something hot is happening here," even when the food itself is room temperature.

That nerve signal can set off a reflex involving your nose. You may sweat, your eyes may water, and glands inside your nose may release extra mucus. The official name for this particular food-triggered drip is gustatory rhinitis. It is a form of nonallergic rhinitis, which means your nose is reacting without the immune-system chain reaction involved in allergies.

Capsaicin does not dissolve well in water, which is why a sip of water may spread the burning feeling around rather than end it. Dairy foods can help because a milk protein called casein is better at pulling capsaicin away from receptors.

Spicy food creates a curious disagreement: your tongue reports a fire, your nervous system hits a reflex switch, and the thermometer calmly insists that nothing is burning.
