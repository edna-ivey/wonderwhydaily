Many cheeses melt before they burn because heat loosens their fat and protein structure before the surface dries out enough to brown or scorch.

Cheese is a mixture of water, fat, proteins, salt, and other compounds. In many melting cheeses, fat begins to soften and liquefy as temperature rises. At the same time, the protein network holding the cheese together loosens enough for the cheese to slump, stretch, or flow.

Moisture slows the trip toward burning. As long as water is still present near the surface, some incoming heat goes into warming that water and turning it into steam. The surface cannot race far past water's boiling point until enough moisture leaves. Once the surface dries, it can get hotter, brown, and eventually scorch.

The exact result depends on the cheese. Moisture, fat, acidity, aging, calcium, and protein structure all matter. Mozzarella can melt and stretch. Aged cheddar may melt but also release oil. Acid-set cheeses such as paneer often hold their shape because their protein networks behave differently under heat.

So cheese does not follow one universal rule. Heating starts several changes at once, and each cheese decides which change wins first: softening, stretching, oiling, browning, or stubbornly staying put.
