A shark does not treat a tooth like a lifelong possession. A tooth is more like equipment that can be replaced when it wears out.

Sharks keep making new teeth throughout their lives. The working teeth sit near the front of the jaw, while replacement teeth develop behind them in rows. When a front tooth breaks, dulls, or falls out, another tooth can move into place.

That system makes sense for an animal that bites slippery fish, tough skin, bones, shells, or struggling prey. Losing a tooth during dinner is not a disaster. It is part of the plan.

Human teeth are anchored in sockets and are meant to last for decades. Shark teeth are attached differently and are replaced again and again. Different shark species also have different tooth shapes, from needle-like gripping teeth to broader crushing teeth.

The result is a jaw that is never really finished. A shark's teeth are less like a fixed set of silverware and more like a supply line, with the next tool already waiting behind the one in use.
