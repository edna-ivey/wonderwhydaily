Air reaching the lungs needs to be warm and humid enough to protect delicate airway surfaces. The nasal lining is rich in blood vessels and mucus-producing tissue. Cold, dry air makes this conditioning job more demanding.

Nerves in the nose stimulate additional watery secretions, which add moisture to the incoming air. Blood flow helps warm it. Meanwhile, warm moisture in exhaled breath can condense when it meets the cooler front of the nose, adding to the liquid that drips out.

This reaction is sometimes called cold-air rhinitis. It does not require a virus or allergy, though either can make symptoms stronger. Some people have more sensitive nasal nerves and notice the effect more than others.

A scarf loosely covering the nose can trap warmer, more humid air and reduce the contrast. The nuisance has a useful purpose: your nose is sacrificing a little comfort at the entrance to protect the airways deeper inside.
