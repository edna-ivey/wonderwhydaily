Early U.S. presidents proclaimed occasional days of thanksgiving, and the dates varied. In 1863, Abraham Lincoln called for a national Thanksgiving on the last Thursday of November. Later presidents continued that custom, but it was still set by proclamation rather than a permanent calendar rule.

In 1939, November had five Thursdays. President Franklin D. Roosevelt moved the holiday to the second-to-last Thursday, hoping a longer shopping season before Christmas would help the economy. Some states followed him while others kept the traditional last Thursday. Families, schools, and football schedules faced two competing dates.

Congress stepped in to create one federal holiday. The House first favored the last Thursday, but the Senate chose the fourth Thursday, which avoids pushing the holiday to November 29 or 30 in a five-Thursday year. Roosevelt signed the measure in December 1941.

That law explains the modern pattern. Thanksgiving can fall from November 22 through November 28, not because it follows an astronomical event, but because lawmakers settled a calendar argument with a rule everyone could use.
