Geese fly in a V largely because birds behind the leader can use rising currents in the wake of wings ahead, reducing the energy needed for a long flight.

A flapping wing pushes air downward, but the wake curls into spinning vortices near the wingtips. Just outside the strongest downward flow is a region of upwash, where air moves upward. A following bird positioned correctly can gain a small lift from that rising air.

Position is only part of the trick. Studies of migrating birds found that followers also adjust the timing of their wingbeats to meet the useful part of a neighbor's swirling wake. If a bird falls into the wrong spot, it changes its rhythm to avoid the downward-moving air.

The lead bird receives no upwash from a bird ahead, so members of a flock may trade positions. A V also gives many birds a clear forward view and space for wingbeats, though real formations shift constantly with wind, fatigue, and social behavior.

One bird's flight leaves an invisible landscape behind it. By arranging themselves across that landscape, the flock turns otherwise wasted moving air into shared help for the journey.
