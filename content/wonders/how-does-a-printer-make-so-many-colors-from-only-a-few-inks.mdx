A printer does not need a separate ink for every color in a photograph. Most color printers work with cyan, magenta, yellow, and black, known as CMYK. Each colored ink absorbs some wavelengths from the white light that hits the paper and reflects the rest toward your eyes.

By placing inks together, the printer can subtract different combinations of light. Cyan and yellow, for example, can produce an area that looks green. Black ink improves dark details, text, and neutral shadows without requiring heavy layers of the three colored inks.

The page is also covered with patterns of extremely small dots. A printer varies dot size, spacing, and overlap in a process called halftoning. From normal reading distance, your visual system does not resolve each dot. It averages the reflected light, making a patch look like one smooth shade.

A screen uses a different strategy. Its pixels emit red, green, and blue light, adding light together. Paper emits no light of its own, so ink must work with the white illumination reflected from the page. That is why a bright screen color can be difficult to reproduce exactly in print.
