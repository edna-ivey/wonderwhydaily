A classic orb web is not one uniform sheet of glue. Its frame and spoke-like radial lines are mainly structural and often dry, while a spiral of capture silk carries sticky droplets. The spider can choose routes that keep much of its body away from the glue.

That is only part of the protection. Close-up studies of orb-weaving spiders found branching hairs on their legs and a chemical coating that reduces adhesion. Glue droplets tend to slide along the hairs instead of spreading across a large surface. When researchers washed away the coating, spider legs stuck more strongly.

Spiders also move with care. They place and pull their legs in ways that reduce contact forces, even while touching sticky threads many times during web construction. No single trick makes them completely immune. A spider can become caught if it is injured, careless, or forced against too much glue.

The web succeeds because it separates jobs. Tough dry silk supports the structure, stretchy sticky silk absorbs a flying insect's energy, and the builder has both a route map and specialized legs for moving through its own trap.
