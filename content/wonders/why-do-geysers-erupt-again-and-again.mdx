Geysers erupt again and again because water and heat recharge a constricted underground plumbing system after each eruption.

Rain and melted snow seep underground, where hot rock warms the water. Deep water is squeezed by the weight of water above it, raising its boiling point. A geyser also needs chambers and narrow passages that slow circulation and let hot water and steam become trapped.

Small bubbles eventually push some water out of the vent. That lowers pressure deeper in the system. Water that was liquid under high pressure can suddenly flash into steam, expanding dramatically and driving a column of water and vapor into the air.

The eruption partly empties and cools the plumbing. Groundwater begins filling it again, hot water rises from below, and the pressure-building process restarts. The timing depends on the shape of hidden passages, heat supply, water supply, recent earthquakes, and mineral deposits that alter the channels.

Old Faithful is fairly predictable, but most geysers are not clockwork. Their repetition comes from a cycle of refill and pressure, while their changing underground pipes decide how regular that cycle becomes.
