An incandescent bulb sends current through a thin tungsten filament. Electrical resistance heats the wire until it glows. The glow includes visible light, but most of the energy leaves as infrared radiation and heat rather than useful illumination.

A light-emitting diode uses a semiconductor junction. When current flows in the correct direction, electrons and electron vacancies recombine. Their energy is released as photons. Engineers choose semiconductor materials and structures that produce particular colors efficiently.

LED lamps still create some heat, and their bases include components that manage power and carry heat away. They are not perfectly efficient. However, they can deliver comparable visible brightness with much less electricity and typically last much longer because no fragile white-hot filament slowly evaporates.

Efficiency is usually compared in lumens of visible light per watt of electrical power. That is why buying by lumens, not old watt expectations, makes sense. Watts describe energy use; lumens describe the light you receive.
