Deleting a file often removes the computer's record of where that file belongs and marks its storage space as available, without immediately replacing every bit of the old data.

A storage device is organized with a kind of index. The operating system uses that index to locate the pieces of each file. When you empty the trash, the system can remove the file's entry and declare those locations free. The old pattern may remain until new information is written over it.

That is why recovery software can sometimes restore recently deleted files. It searches for recognizable data in space the computer now calls unused. Recovery becomes less likely as the device continues working and writes new information into those locations.

Modern solid-state drives complicate the story. They move data around to spread wear, and a command called TRIM can tell the drive which blocks are no longer needed. Encryption also changes secure deletion: destroying a strong encryption key can make remaining encrypted bits unreadable. The correct sanitizing method depends on the device and the risk.

For everyday use, the trash protects against accidents. For sensitive data, deletion and secure erasure are different jobs, because losing a file's address is not always the same as destroying its contents.
