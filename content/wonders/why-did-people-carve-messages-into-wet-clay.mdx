People in ancient Mesopotamia wrote on wet clay because it was plentiful, inexpensive, easy to mark with a reed, and durable after drying.

Growing cities needed records of grain, animals, labor, taxes, and trade. River valleys supplied clay close at hand. A scribe could shape a palm-sized tablet, smooth its surface, and press signs into it while it remained damp. Early pictorial marks gradually developed into wedge-shaped cuneiform signs.

Clay offered useful choices. A temporary note could be recycled by wetting and reshaping it. An important tablet could dry hard, be fired deliberately, or become baked during a building fire. Some messages were enclosed in clay envelopes whose seals showed whether anyone had tampered with them.

The material also influenced the script. Drawing curves in sticky clay was awkward, while pressing the angled tip of a cut reed made quick wedges. Over time, writers combined hundreds of signs to represent words, syllables, names, and grammar in several languages.

Paper may feel more natural to us, but clay gave early urban societies a local storage technology. Soft enough for a message today, it could become hard enough for archaeologists to read thousands of years later.
