The Moon sometimes turns red during a total lunar eclipse because Earth's atmosphere filters sunlight and bends the remaining red and orange light into our planet's shadow.

A lunar eclipse happens when the full Moon passes through Earth's shadow. Earth blocks the direct sunlight that normally brightens the lunar surface. Yet the shadow is not completely black because a thin ring of atmosphere surrounds our planet.

Sunlight skimming that atmosphere follows a long path. Molecules scatter shorter blue wavelengths more easily, the same process that helps make the daytime sky blue and sunsets red. The atmosphere also refracts, or bends, some of the filtered reddish light inward until it reaches the Moon.

Dust, smoke, clouds, and the Moon's path through the shadow affect the exact color and brightness. One eclipse may look coppery orange while another is deep red or unusually dark. Lunar eclipses do not happen every full Moon because the Moon's orbit is tilted about five degrees.

From the lunar surface, the event would look extraordinary: Earth covering the Sun, surrounded by a glowing red ring made from sunrises and sunsets all the way around the planet.
