You can sometimes smell rain before it reaches you because moving air carries chemicals from the storm and from ground that has already become wet nearby.

One earthy ingredient is geosmin, made by soil microorganisms. During dry weather it can collect in soil and on surfaces. When raindrops strike porous ground, tiny air bubbles burst and launch aerosol droplets containing geosmin and plant oils. Humans are extremely sensitive to that compound.

Thunderstorms can add a sharper smell. Electrical activity and atmospheric chemistry produce ozone, while downdrafts carry air from higher levels toward the ground. Gusts spreading out from a storm may deliver that scent before the main curtain of rain arrives at your location.

So the smell is not a prediction made by the nose from nothing. Rain may already be falling upwind, and its air is moving faster or farther than the drops crossing your exact spot. Humidity can also change how odors travel and how strongly we notice them.

The familiar scent called petrichor is a chemical message from soil, microbes, plants, and moving weather. Your nose receives the first airborne part of the storm before your skin receives the water.
