Hot water can sometimes freeze before cooler water because heating changes several parts of the experiment, but it does not happen under every set of conditions.

The puzzle is called the Mpemba effect. Evaporation can remove some of the warmer sample, leaving less water to freeze. Strong convection can carry heat to the surface efficiently. Hot water may contain fewer dissolved gases, and the samples may supercool by different amounts before ice crystals begin forming.

The surroundings can change too. A hot container might melt frost beneath it and make better thermal contact with a freezer shelf. Container shape, water purity, airflow, starting temperature, and whether 'freezing' means the first crystal or a completely solid block can all change the winner.

That sensitivity explains why careful experiments disagree. In ordinary matched conditions, cooler water usually has the obvious advantage because it has less heat to lose. A claim that hot water always freezes faster is false, and scientists still debate which mechanisms dominate in particular observations.

The effect is a lesson in experimental thinking. Two cups labeled hot and cold may differ in many ways by the time ice appears, so a surprising result should begin a question, not end one.
