A metal spoon feels colder than a wooden utensil at the same room temperature because metal carries heat away from your warm skin much faster.

Your temperature sense responds strongly to the rate of heat flow. Skin is usually warmer than room-temperature objects, so energy moves from your hand into whatever you touch. Metal has high thermal conductivity, allowing that energy to spread rapidly through the spoon. The skin at the contact point cools quickly and reports cold.

Wood is a poor conductor. Its structure traps air and slows energy transfer, so the surface touching you warms while heat moves only gradually deeper into the utensil. Your skin temperature changes less, and the wood feels warmer even when a thermometer says both objects began alike.

Reverse the temperatures and the sensation reverses too. If metal and wood were both safely warmer than your skin, metal would feel hotter because it would deliver energy faster. This is one reason metal cookware needs insulated handles.

Touch is not a laboratory thermometer reading an object's temperature in isolation. It is measuring an interaction, especially how quickly energy crosses the boundary between the material and you.
