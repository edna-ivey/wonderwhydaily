Before standard time, many towns set noon for the moment when the Sun reached its highest point in their own sky. Because Earth turns continuously, solar noon arrives a little later as you travel west. That did not matter much when most journeys were slow, but it created a jumble of clock settings across a large country.

Railroads made the problem urgent. Trains crossed many local-time boundaries in a single trip, and stations sometimes displayed several clocks for different rail lines. Confusing times made schedules hard to read and could contribute to dangerous mistakes. Telegraph networks also needed a dependable way to state when something happened far away.

In 1883, major North American railroads adopted coordinated regional zones. Rather than every town following its exact solar time, places across a broad band used the same clock. The United States later placed standard time into federal law and assigned responsibility for official boundaries.

The zones are based loosely on longitude because Earth rotates 360 degrees in about 24 hours, or roughly 15 degrees per hour. Real borders bend around states, countries, communities, and practical needs. Time zones are therefore part astronomy and part human agreement.
