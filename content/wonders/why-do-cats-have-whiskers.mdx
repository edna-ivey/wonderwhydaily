Cats have whiskers because these specialized hairs are sensitive touch instruments that help them detect nearby objects, openings, and movements in the air.

A whisker, or vibrissa, is thicker and more deeply rooted than ordinary fur. Its follicle is surrounded by nerves and blood-filled tissue. When the shaft bends even slightly, the follicle changes shape and sensory nerves send information to the brain.

Whiskers are especially useful close to the face, where a cat's eyes cannot focus well. They can brush an edge before the nose arrives, help locate prey during a close pounce, and detect subtle air currents around obstacles. Their position can also change with attention, fear, or relaxation, adding to body language.

The popular claim that whiskers always equal a cat's exact body width is too simple. Body size changes, fur adds bulk, and openings vary. Whiskers can help investigate a gap, but a cat still combines that input with vision, hearing, memory, and body movement.

Cutting a whisker does not remove its nerve, but it removes the long lever that supplies useful signals until the hair regrows. A cat's whiskers deserve the same hands-off respect as any sensory equipment.
