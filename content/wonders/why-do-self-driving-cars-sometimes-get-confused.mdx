Self-driving systems sometimes get confused because they must turn imperfect sensor data into a correct understanding of a road scene, then predict what will happen and choose a safe action.

Cameras, radar, lidar, ultrasonic sensors, satellite positioning, and maps each provide different clues. Software combines them to identify lanes, signs, vehicles, bicycles, people, and open space. Rain, snow, glare, darkness, dirty sensors, faded markings, construction, or an object unlike the training examples can make those clues incomplete or contradictory.

Detection is only the beginning. The system must decide whether a person near the curb will cross, whether a stopped vehicle will move, and whether a hand signal overrides a traffic light. Human behavior is variable, and rare situations are difficult to represent in testing data.

Different vehicles automate different tasks. Driver-assistance features may steer or control speed but still require an attentive human. NHTSA says fully automated consumer vehicles that handle every road and condition without a human fallback are not currently for sale in the United States.

A machine can react quickly and monitor every direction, yet safe driving demands reliable judgment about an open-ended world. Confusion appears where uncertain sensing meets a decision that cannot wait.
