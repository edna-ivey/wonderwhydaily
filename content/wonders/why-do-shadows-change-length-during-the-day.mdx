A shadow forms where an object blocks light. The shape on the ground depends on the direction of the incoming rays. Early and late in the day, the Sun appears low near the horizon, so light arrives at a shallow angle. The blocked region stretches a long distance behind the object.

As Earth rotates, the Sun appears to climb higher. Around local solar noon, its rays strike the ground more steeply, and a standing object's shadow is usually at its shortest. The shadow then lengthens in the opposite direction as the Sun sinks toward sunset.

The exact pattern changes with latitude and season. The Sun follows a higher path across a summer sky and a lower path in winter, so even the shortest shadow at noon has different lengths during the year. Near the equator on certain dates, the Sun can be almost overhead and a vertical object's shadow nearly vanishes.

People used this dependable motion in sundials. A carefully positioned pointer casts a shadow across hour marks, turning Earth's rotation into a clock you can read on the ground.
