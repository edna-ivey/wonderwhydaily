Sinkholes are common in places underlain by rock that groundwater can dissolve, especially limestone, dolomite, gypsum, or salt. Rainwater absorbs carbon dioxide from air and soil, becoming weakly acidic. As it moves through cracks, it slowly enlarges them into channels and cavities.

Some sinkholes form gradually as loose sediment settles into openings below. Others remain hidden because clay or rock creates a temporary roof over a growing void. The surface may look ordinary until that roof becomes too thin or heavy to hold itself up. Then it can collapse over hours or even moments.

Rainfall changes, leaking pipes, construction, and groundwater pumping can influence the timing. Pumping may lower underground water levels that helped support sediment, while added water can wash soil into cavities. That does not mean every ground depression is a sinkhole, and predicting an exact collapse can be difficult.

The dramatic hole is only the final visible step. The main process usually began long before, as moving water quietly carried dissolved rock and loose particles away beneath the surface.
