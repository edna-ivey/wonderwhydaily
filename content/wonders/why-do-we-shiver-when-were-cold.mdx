We shiver when cold because rapid, involuntary muscle contractions convert chemical energy into heat and help defend the body's core temperature.

Temperature sensors in the skin and deeper body send information to the hypothalamus, a brain region involved in temperature control. The body first reduces heat loss by narrowing blood vessels near the skin and changing behavior. If that is not enough, nerve circuits recruit skeletal muscles to contract and relax repeatedly.

Muscle movement is not perfectly efficient at turning fuel into useful motion. Much of the energy becomes heat. During exercise that heat can make us sweat; during shivering it is the point. Large muscles in the torso often join first, followed by muscles in the limbs as cold stress increases.

Shivering costs energy and eventually tires the muscles. It is an emergency defense, not a substitute for dry clothing and shelter. Violent shivering, confusion, clumsiness, or shivering that stops despite continued cold can signal dangerous hypothermia and needs urgent adult and medical attention.

The shakes may feel unhelpful because they do not move you anywhere. Internally, though, your muscles are running a rapid heat-making workout that the brain started automatically.
