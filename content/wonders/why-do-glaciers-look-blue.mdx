Glaciers look blue because thick, dense ice absorbs red wavelengths of white light more strongly while blue wavelengths travel farther and scatter back to our eyes.

White sunlight contains a range of colors. In a thin ice cube, light does not travel far enough for the difference to become dramatic. Inside a glacier or deep crevasse, the path can be many meters long. The small preference adds up until much of the red light is gone.

Old glacier ice is also compressed. Snow layers accumulate, grains merge, and many air bubbles are squeezed out. Fewer ice-air boundaries mean less of the broad scattering that makes fresh snow look white. Light can penetrate more deeply into the ice before returning.

Not every glacier surface looks blue. Cracks, bubbles, fresh snow, sediment, algae, and lighting can make ice appear white, gray, green, brown, or nearly black. The strongest blue often appears where clean dense ice is exposed or where light passes through a thick wall.

The glacier does not contain a bucket of blue dye and is not merely mirroring the sky. Its color is the result left behind after a long journey removes more of the warmer-colored light.
