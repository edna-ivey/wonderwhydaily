A few people can wiggle their ears because they can deliberately activate small muscles around the outer ear.

Those muscles are called auricular muscles. In many mammals, similar muscles help turn the ears toward sounds. Humans still have them, but they usually do very little visible work. Most of us turn our whole head instead.

Some people can consciously contract these muscles. Others may discover a tiny movement when they raise their eyebrows, tense their scalp, or make certain facial expressions. The ear is not flapping by itself; nearby muscles are tugging the outer ear just enough to move it.

That does not mean ear wigglers hear dramatically better. Human ears cannot swivel with the precision of a cat's ears, and the movement is usually too small to change hearing much.

Ear wiggling is a reminder that bodies carry old equipment in new circumstances. The muscles are still there. A few people just have enough control to make the old hardware visibly twitch.
