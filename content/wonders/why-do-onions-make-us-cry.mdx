Onions make us cry because cutting them starts a chemical chain that releases an irritating vapor, and our eyes produce tears to wash it away.

An onion stores different chemical ingredients in separate parts of its cells. When a knife slices through, enzymes meet sulfur-containing compounds. The reactions produce a volatile substance called lachrymatory factor. Because it easily becomes a vapor, it drifts upward from the cutting board.

When that vapor reaches the wet surface of an eye, sensory nerves detect irritation. The nervous system tells the lacrimal glands above the eyes to make more tears. Blinking spreads the extra fluid, dilutes the irritant, and helps carry it toward the tear ducts and out of the eye.

A sharp knife crushes fewer cells than a dull one, so it may release less of the chemical machinery. Chilling an onion can slow reactions and reduce how quickly compounds escape. Ventilation can move the vapor away. Goggles work for the simplest reason: they block its path to the eyes.

The onion is not making you sad, and the tears are not a culinary opinion. They are a fast protective wash triggered by chemistry from damaged plant cells.
