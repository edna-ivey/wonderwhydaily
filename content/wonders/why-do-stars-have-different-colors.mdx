Stars emit a range of wavelengths because their dense hot surfaces behave approximately like thermal radiators. Temperature changes the distribution. A hotter surface produces proportionally more short-wavelength light, making a star look blue-white. A cooler one produces more long-wavelength light and appears orange or red.

Our Sun, with a surface near 5,500 degrees Celsius, emits strongly across visible wavelengths and appears essentially white from space. Earth's atmosphere and human color perception can alter how stars look from the ground, especially near the horizon.

Dust between stars can scatter shorter wavelengths and make a distant star appear redder, so astronomers account for that effect. They also spread starlight into spectra, where absorption lines reveal composition while the overall pattern helps estimate temperature.

The colors are subtle because stars are point-like and often not bright enough to strongly stimulate the eye's color receptors. Photographs and slightly defocused views can make the differences easier to notice.
