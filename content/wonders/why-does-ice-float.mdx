Whether something floats depends on density, the amount of mass packed into a given volume. A solid piece of most substances is denser than its liquid form, so it sinks. Water behaves differently because of how its bent molecules attract one another.

Each water molecule can form hydrogen bonds with neighbors. In liquid water, those bonds constantly break and reform, allowing molecules to crowd fairly close. During freezing, the bonds organize molecules into a stable crystal lattice with open spaces.

The same number of molecules now occupies more room, so ice is less dense than liquid water. Buoyant force can support it at the surface, with most of an ice cube below the waterline and a smaller portion above it.

This oddity matters far beyond a drink glass. Floating ice forms a lid that slows heat loss from lakes and seas. If ice sank, water bodies could freeze from the bottom upward much more readily. Water's open crystal structure helps leave liquid habitat beneath the winter surface.
