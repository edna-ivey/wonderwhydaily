In a dim room, pupils widen so more available light can reach the retina. A camera flash placed close to the lens sends an intense burst along nearly the same direction the camera is looking. Some light enters the pupil and reaches the back of the eye.

The retina and the vascular layer behind it contain abundant blood. Light reflected back through the pupil can therefore have a reddish color. Because the flash and camera lens are close together, that returning light travels straight into the photograph.

Distance helps explain why red-eye often appears in dark indoor snapshots. A farther camera makes the angle between flash and lens smaller from the subject's point of view. Moving the flash away from the lens sends the reflection elsewhere. A preflash can trigger pupil constriction before the picture.

Red-eye is an optical effect, not the iris or pupil changing color. Photographers reduce it by adding room light, changing flash position, using bounce flash, or applying careful image correction afterward.
