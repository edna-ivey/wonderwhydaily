A volcano is not a mountain that catches fire. It is a place where material from inside Earth can reach the surface.

Below the crust, melted or partly melted rock is called magma. Magma can rise because it is less dense than the solid rock around it, and because gases dissolved inside it help drive it upward. As magma rises, pressure from the rock above decreases. The gases can expand, a little like bubbles forming when a fizzy drink is opened.

If magma and gas find cracks or weak places in the crust, they can push upward and erupt. What happens next depends a lot on the magma. Runny magma lets gas escape more easily, so lava may flow out in glowing streams. Thick, sticky magma can trap gas until pressure builds, then the eruption can blast ash, rock, and fragments into the air.

That is why volcanoes do not all behave alike. The ingredients can be similar, but the texture of the magma, the amount of gas, and the shape of the underground path can change the eruption completely.

An eruption can destroy a landscape, but it can also build one. Layer after layer of lava and ash can create new ground, new mountains, and even new islands.
