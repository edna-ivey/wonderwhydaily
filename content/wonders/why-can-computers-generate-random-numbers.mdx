Computers usually generate random-looking numbers by running a precise algorithm from a starting value called a seed, producing what scientists call pseudorandom numbers.

A pseudorandom number generator is deterministic. If you know its algorithm and exact internal state, you can reproduce its sequence. But a well-designed generator spreads tiny changes in the seed through its output so thoroughly that the numbers pass statistical tests and are effectively unpredictable to someone who lacks that secret state.

Where does the seed come from? Operating systems collect entropy from hard-to-predict physical timings and hardware events, such as fine variations in device activity. Some specialized generators measure electronic noise, radioactive events, or quantum behavior. Those real-world inputs help prevent the same sequence from appearing every time.

Different jobs need different standards. A simple game may tolerate a fast generator that only looks varied. Encryption keys need a cryptographically secure generator designed so observing past output does not reveal future values. A poor generator or predictable seed can break an otherwise strong security system.

Computers do not suspend logic to roll an imaginary die. They combine carefully tested mathematics with uncertainty gathered from the physical world, creating results that are useful precisely because outsiders cannot predict them.
