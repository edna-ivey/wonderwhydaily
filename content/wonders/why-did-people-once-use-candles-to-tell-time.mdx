People used candle clocks because a candle made to burn fairly steadily becomes shorter in a visible, one-way pattern that can be marked to estimate elapsed time.

The maker divided the candle into sections. If tests showed that one section usually burned in an hour, the flame reaching the next mark signaled that roughly an hour had passed. Unlike a sundial, the device could work indoors, at night, or under a cloudy sky.

Some versions turned measurement into an alarm. A small nail or metal ball was pushed into the wax beside a mark. When the flame melted down to that level, the object dropped onto a metal plate with a sound. The idea was clever, though not as precise as a good mechanical clock.

Wax composition, wick size, drafts, temperature, and the candle's shape all affect burning speed. A candle clock therefore measured duration better than it identified one exact universal time. It was useful for dividing a night, timing prayers, or organizing household tasks.

Every clock needs something that changes at a dependable rate. Modern clocks count electronic vibrations. The candle clock made the same basic bargain with a much slower process: let steady change become a ruler for time.
