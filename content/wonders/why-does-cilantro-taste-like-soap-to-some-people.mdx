Cilantro can taste soapy because soap-like aroma molecules stand out more strongly to some people.

Much of flavor comes from smell. Cilantro releases several aroma molecules called aldehydes. Some aldehydes smell green or citrusy; others resemble compounds found in soaps and lotions. If your smell receptors are especially tuned to those notes, cilantro can taste less like a fresh herb and more like someone made a terrible garnish decision.

Genetics appears to play a role. Researchers have found an association near odor-receptor genes, including one involved in detecting aldehydes. That does not mean there is a single "cilantro gene" that decides your taco future forever. Culture, repeated exposure, freshness, and the other ingredients in a dish can all influence whether cilantro tastes pleasant.

Some people who dislike cilantro become more comfortable with it over time, especially when it is chopped, blended, or mixed with other strong flavors. The leaf has not changed into soap. Your sensory system is simply highlighting a different part of its chemical mixture.

Two people can eat the same bite while their noses tell very different stories.
