Astronauts sometimes see flashes with closed eyes because high-energy cosmic particles pass through the eye or visual tissue and trigger signals that the brain interprets as light.

Cosmic rays are fast atomic nuclei and other particles arriving from energetic events in space. Earth's atmosphere and magnetic field block or deflect many of them at the surface. Beyond much of that protection, more particles can pass through a spacecraft and an astronaut.

Apollo astronauts reported spots, streaks, and cloudy flashes in darkness. Experiments tracked particles crossing detectors near their heads, and laboratory beams produced similar sensations when high-energy ions passed through the eye. The interaction may directly excite retinal cells or create brief light within the eye, with different mechanisms contributing to different flashes.

Closed eyelids do not matter because the event begins behind them. The visual system only knows that retinal or nerve signals arrived; it does not require a photon to have traveled through the pupil in the usual way. Scientists call light sensations without external light phosphenes.

The flashes turned astronauts themselves into tiny particle detectors. A silent visitor from deep space crossed living tissue, and the brain translated that invisible event into something the person could see.
