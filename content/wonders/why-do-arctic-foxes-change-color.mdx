Many arctic foxes wear thick white fur in winter and a shorter brown or gray coat in summer. Existing hairs do not suddenly switch pigment. The fox sheds one coat and grows another through a seasonal molt.

Day length is a dependable environmental signal. Changes detected by the eyes influence hormones that control hair growth and pigmentation. Temperature and other conditions can affect timing, but photoperiod helps begin the process before the landscape has completely changed.

White winter fur reduces contrast against snow and sea ice, helping a fox approach prey and avoid larger predators. Darker summer fur matches exposed tundra, rocks, and soil. The winter coat is also exceptionally dense, providing insulation rather than camouflage alone.

Nature includes variation. Blue-morph arctic foxes remain charcoal, brown, or bluish gray in winter. Different color strategies can work better in different habitats, showing that seasonal camouflage is an adaptation, not a rigid costume rule.
