Modern cameras can record an image in a tiny fraction of a second because electronic sensors respond quickly and lenses gather light efficiently. Early photography relied on metal plates or other surfaces coated with chemicals that changed much more slowly when light struck them.

In the first years of the daguerreotype, a prepared silver-plated copper plate might need three to fifteen minutes of exposure. The photographer removed a lens cap to begin, waited while light built a hidden chemical image, then developed the plate using a careful sequence that included mercury vapor and a fixing bath.

Long exposures shaped what could be photographed. Portrait subjects had to remain extremely still, sometimes with hidden head supports. Busy streets could look strangely empty because moving people did not stay in one place long enough to register clearly. Action scenes were nearly impossible.

Chemists improved the sensitivity of plates, and lens makers learned to collect more light. Exposure times dropped below a minute and eventually to seconds and fractions of seconds. Photography began freezing expressions and motion that its earliest practitioners could only watch pass by.
