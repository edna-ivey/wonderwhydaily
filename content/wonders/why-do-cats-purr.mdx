A purring cat sounds as if it has a small motor tucked somewhere inside. But the better question is not only how cats make that sound. It is what the sound is for.

There is no single translation for every purr. Cats may purr when they are relaxed, but they can also purr when they want attention, when they are hungry, when they are stressed, during birth, or when they are hurt. The same sound can show up in very different situations.

That makes purring less like one word and more like a tool. Sometimes it may signal comfort. Sometimes it may ask for care. Sometimes it may help a cat calm itself. Scientists are still careful about assigning one universal meaning, because cats do not purr only when everything is cozy.

The sound itself is produced in the voice box, or larynx. For a long time, scientists explained purring as rapid muscle activity around the vocal folds as air moves through. Newer research suggests the folds have special fatty pads that can help them vibrate at purring frequencies.

So a purr is both a sound and a situation. A sleepy cat on your lap is one version. A worried cat at the vet may be another. The rumble is real; the meaning depends on the rest of the cat.
