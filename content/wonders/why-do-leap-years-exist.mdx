A calendar year has 365 days, but Earth does not complete one orbit around the Sun in exactly that time. The trip takes about 365.2422 days. That extra fraction seems small, yet it would push the calendar almost one day out of step with the seasons after four years.

Adding February 29 catches up most of that drift. The familiar rule says years divisible by four are leap years. That correction is slightly too large, though, so the Gregorian calendar uses two more rules: century years are usually not leap years, but century years divisible by 400 are. This keeps the average calendar year very close to the astronomical one.

Without corrections, dates tied to the seasons would slowly wander. Over many centuries, a date called the first day of spring could arrive while the actual seasonal cycle was somewhere else. Leap day is less a bonus day than careful calendar maintenance.

The system is not mathematically perfect forever, because Earth's rotation and orbit can change by tiny amounts. For ordinary life, however, it keeps our months impressively well matched to Earth's journey around the Sun.
