Ice at its melting point sits in balance with liquid water. Adding salt disrupts that balance by lowering the temperature at which the salty water can freeze. At zero degrees Celsius, more ice must melt to create the new saltwater mixture.

Melting requires energy. The ice absorbs that energy as heat from its surroundings, including the cream in an inner container. As the ice-salt bath loses thermal energy, its temperature can drop below the ordinary freezing point of pure water.

The cream mixture gives up heat to the colder bath and begins freezing. Stirring prevents large ice crystals from growing and incorporates air, creating a smoother texture. The salt belongs outside the dessert unless the recipe calls for a small amount for flavor.

Salt does not make cold from nothing. It changes the phase balance so ice can melt at a lower temperature, and the energy needed for that phase change comes from nearby material.
