Your stomach can growl without hunger because the sound comes from muscular movement of gas and liquid through the stomach and intestines, not from an empty-tank sensor.

The digestive tract has layers of smooth muscle that squeeze in coordinated waves. Those contractions mix food, move it forward, and sweep material between meals. Gas and fluid shifting through narrow and wider spaces create vibrations called borborygmi. A mostly empty gut may sound louder because there is less solid material to muffle them.

Between meals, a repeating pattern called the migrating motor complex sends stronger waves through the stomach and small intestine. It helps clear leftover material and bacteria toward the large intestine. This activity often overlaps with hunger signals, which is why rumbling and appetite are commonly linked.

But the two are not identical. Digestion after a meal, swallowed air, stress, body position, and ordinary variations in gut movement can all make noise. Hunger itself involves hormones, nerves, energy needs, habits, and signals between the digestive tract and brain.

A growl is evidence that a flexible tube full of moving material is doing mechanical work. It may accompany hunger, but it is not a reliable command that you must eat at that instant.
