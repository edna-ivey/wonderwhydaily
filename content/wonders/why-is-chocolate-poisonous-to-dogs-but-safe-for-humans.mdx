Chocolate can poison dogs because it contains theobromine and caffeine, stimulants that a dog's body breaks down much more slowly than a human body does.

Both chemicals belong to a family called methylxanthines. People can usually process the amounts found in an ordinary serving of chocolate before they build to dangerous levels. In dogs, theobromine stays in circulation longer. Enough of it can overstimulate the heart, nervous system, and other organs.

Risk depends on the dog's size, the amount eaten, and the kind of chocolate. Cocoa powder, baking chocolate, and dark chocolate are especially concentrated. Milk chocolate contains less, and white chocolate contains very little theobromine, though its fat and sugar can still make a pet ill.

Symptoms may include vomiting, restlessness, a rapid heartbeat, tremors, or seizures. This is not a situation for a home experiment or a wait-and-see guess. If a dog eats chocolate, an adult should promptly contact a veterinarian or animal poison service and report the type, amount, time, and dog's weight.

A food being safe for one species does not make it safe for another. The important difference is often how each body's enzymes absorb, transform, and remove the same chemical.
