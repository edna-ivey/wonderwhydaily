A polar bear's outer hairs are not colored with white pigment. They are nearly transparent and have a complex structure with air spaces. When light enters the coat, it reflects and scatters among many hairs. The visible wavelengths mix together in our eyes as white.

That pale appearance provides camouflage across sea ice and snow. The coat can also take on other tints. Warm sunlight may make it look creamy or yellow, while algae growing in hairs has occasionally given captive bears a greenish cast.

Beneath the fur is dark skin. The bear also has dense underfur and a thick fat layer, which are crucial insulation. The hairs trap air and reduce heat loss, though wet fur insulates less effectively than a dry coat.

The common claim that every hair works like a fiber-optic cable funneling sunlight to the skin is an oversimplification. The well-supported explanation for the color is structural scattering. The coat's appearance and its insulation come from physical design, not white dye.
