Snowflakes have six sides because water molecules in ordinary ice arrange themselves in a crystal structure with sixfold symmetry.

A snow crystal begins high in a cloud when water vapor attaches to a tiny particle and freezes. Each water molecule has one oxygen atom and two hydrogen atoms. Electrical attractions between molecules guide them into an open hexagonal network. As more vapor joins, that molecular geometry appears in the crystal's larger shape.

The six arms grow at the same time and pass through nearly the same air, so they tend to echo one another. Yet a flake tumbles through regions with changing temperature and humidity. Tiny shifts alter how quickly the edges and corners grow, producing plates, columns, needles, or elaborate branches.

The arms are similar, but they are not perfectly identical. Minute differences at each growing tip accumulate, and every flake follows its own path through the cloud. That makes exact duplicates extraordinarily unlikely even though all begin with the same molecular rules.

A snowflake is a record of two things at once: the dependable geometry of ice and the chaotic trip of one crystal through a changing cloud. Sixfold order provides the framework; weather writes the decoration.
