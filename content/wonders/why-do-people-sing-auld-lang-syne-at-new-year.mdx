The phrase auld lang syne is Scots and refers to old times or days gone by. Poet Robert Burns sent a version of the song to a collector in 1788, saying he had gathered it from an older person's singing. Burns shaped material rooted in earlier oral tradition.

Its verses ask whether old acquaintances should be forgotten, then answer with memories, goodwill, and a shared cup. Those ideas fit Hogmanay, Scotland's New Year celebration, when communities looked back together and welcomed what came next.

The song traveled through Scottish communities and English-language songbooks. In North America, bandleader Guy Lombardo's New Year's Eve performances and radio and television broadcasts helped make its melody a mass-media signal for midnight.

Many singers know only the opening lines, and versions vary. The tradition lasts because the emotional idea is simple: pause at a boundary, remember people and experiences, and carry connection into the new year.
