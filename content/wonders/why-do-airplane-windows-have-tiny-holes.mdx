The tiny hole in an airplane window helps equalize pressure between panes so the strongest outer structural pane carries the main difference between cabin air and the thin air outside.

A passenger window is an assembly of layers. The innermost scratch pane protects the structural window from passengers. Farther out are strong transparent panes designed for repeated pressurization. As the airplane climbs, cabin pressure remains much higher than outside pressure, pushing outward across every window.

The visible breather, or bleed, hole opens the space between structural panes to cabin pressure. That means nearly the full pressure difference develops across the outer pane, where designers intend it. A middle structural pane remains available as a backup if the outer one fails.

Slow airflow through the hole also helps moisture leave the gap, reducing fogging and frost between layers. It does not open a tunnel through the fuselage. Its diameter is tiny, and the sealed outer pane still separates cabin air from the atmosphere.

What looks like a flaw is a carefully placed control. By giving trapped air a route to equalize, engineers decide where a huge pressure load goes and preserve a second line of defense.
