Swiss-style cheeses begin with milk, starter cultures, and a sequence of curd-making steps. During aging, particular bacteria consume compounds in the cheese and release carbon dioxide gas. The gas cannot escape easily through the firming curd, so it gathers into bubbles.

As each bubble expands, it pushes the surrounding cheese aside. The curd continues to mature around the pocket, preserving a rounded opening that cheesemakers call an eye. This is similar to gas making holes in bread, except bread relies mainly on yeast and expands in the oven while Swiss cheese develops its eyes slowly during warm aging.

The size and number of eyes depend on more than one microbe. Temperature, moisture, acidity, salt, and the texture of the curd all affect how much gas forms and where it can collect. Cheesemakers adjust these conditions to create a consistent interior instead of random cracks.

The same bacteria also help build the cheese's nutty, slightly sweet flavor. The holes are therefore not defects or missing pieces. They are visible leftovers from the fermentation chemistry that helps turn milk into Swiss cheese.
