Early postal charges could depend on distance and the number of sheets, creating complicated fees. In Britain, the recipient often paid when a letter arrived. A person could refuse an expensive letter, leaving the postal service with the cost of carrying it.

Reformer Rowland Hill argued for a low, uniform rate paid by the sender. A small adhesive label could prove payment. Britain introduced the Penny Black in 1840, allowing a letter of a standard weight to travel for one penny regardless of distance within the country.

The system simplified sorting and encouraged ordinary people to send more mail. Other nations adopted adhesive stamps, which also became miniature pieces of public art and national identity. Postmarks prevented easy reuse by canceling the stamp.

A stamp did not merely decorate an envelope. It represented a new agreement: the journey was paid for in advance, the rate was understandable, and the recipient could accept the message without facing a surprise delivery charge.
