Marshmallows puff up in a microwave because gas trapped in their many bubbles warms and expands while heat softens the sugary walls around those bubbles.

A marshmallow is a foam. During manufacturing, air is whipped into a mixture of sugar, water, and gelatin. When the mixture cools, the gelatin and concentrated sugar form a flexible network around thousands of tiny air pockets.

Microwaves are absorbed especially well by water molecules in the candy. As the marshmallow heats, its structure softens. The air in each pocket becomes warmer, and some water becomes vapor. Faster-moving gas molecules push outward, stretching the now-soft bubble walls so the whole marshmallow swells.

Heating longer does not create endless growth. Water escapes, sugar becomes extremely hot, and thin bubble walls can rupture. After the microwave stops, cooling gas contracts and vapor condenses, so the marshmallow shrinks. Its altered structure may harden into a chewy or crisp shape.

The expansion looks like a new marshmallow appearing from nowhere, but nearly all the space was present from the start. Heat simply made an existing network of microscopic bubbles reveal how much room it could occupy.
