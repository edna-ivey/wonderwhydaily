Every exhale contains water vapor picked up from the moist surfaces of your lungs and airways. Water vapor is a gas, so individual molecules are too small to see. In warm air, much of that water can remain in gaseous form.

On a cold day, the warm breath rapidly mixes with chilly surroundings. Cooling lowers the amount of water vapor the air can support before it becomes saturated. Some vapor condenses onto microscopic particles, forming countless tiny liquid droplets. In very cold conditions, some may freeze into ice crystals.

Those particles scatter light, so together they look like a pale cloud. The cloud vanishes as the droplets spread out and evaporate into drier air. Temperature and humidity determine how large and persistent it becomes.

This is related to cloud and fog formation. In each case, moist air cools until water changes from an invisible gas into visible suspended particles. Your breath simply creates the process on a scale small enough to carry with you.
