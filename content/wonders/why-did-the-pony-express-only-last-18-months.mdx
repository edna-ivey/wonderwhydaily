The Pony Express lasted only about a year and a half because the transcontinental telegraph made its daring horseback message relay obsolete almost as soon as it became famous.

The service began in April 1860. Riders carried mail along a nearly 2,000-mile route between St. Joseph, Missouri, and Sacramento, California, changing horses at frequent stations. Relays could move a letter across the West in roughly ten days, an astonishing improvement over many earlier routes.

But the Pony Express was expensive and never became a comfortable, dependable passenger-style business. It carried small amounts of high-priority mail, and riders faced severe weather, rough terrain, and danger. Meanwhile, crews were rapidly extending telegraph lines from both sides of the continent.

On October 24, 1861, the first transcontinental telegraph was completed. A message that took riders days could now be transmitted electrically in minutes. The Pony Express officially closed two days later. Depending on which endpoints historians use, its run is described as about 18 or 19 months.

The riders became symbols of speed and courage, but their greatest rival was not a faster horse. It was a new way to separate information from the physical object carrying it.
