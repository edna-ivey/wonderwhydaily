Dolphins sleep using something called unihemispheric sleep. That enormous word means a surprisingly simple thing: one hemisphere, or half, of the brain sleeps while the other half stays awake enough to handle the important stuff.

And for a dolphin, the important stuff includes breathing. Dolphins are mammals, so they breathe air through a blowhole. They do not automatically keep breathing underwater the way a fish keeps water moving over its gills. A dolphin has to surface on purpose, even when it is resting.

The awake half also helps the dolphin keep moving, stay near its pod, and notice danger. The eye connected to the awake side is usually the open one, so a sleeping dolphin can look strangely alert because, in a very real sense, it is.

Then the sides trade jobs. One half wakes up, the other gets its turn to rest, and the open eye can switch too.

So this is not a spooky dolphin habit or a permanent wink. It is a survival trick for an animal that lives in water, breathes air, and cannot afford to let the whole control room go dark at once.
