The eardrum separates the outer ear from an air-filled space called the middle ear. A narrow passage called the Eustachian tube connects that space to the back of the nose and throat. The tube is usually closed, which means pressure inside the middle ear cannot change instantly.

As an airplane climbs, cabin pressure decreases. During descent it increases again. If pressure on one side of the eardrum differs from the other, the membrane bends and hearing may feel muffled or uncomfortable. When the Eustachian tube opens, air moves until the pressures are closer to equal. That sudden adjustment can sound and feel like a pop.

Swallowing, chewing, and yawning activate muscles that help open the tube. Descending is often harder because the increasing outside pressure can squeeze the passage closed. Congestion from a cold or allergies may narrow it further, which is why flying while stuffy can be painful.

The sensation is mechanical, not an explosion inside the ear. The pop means the pressure-balancing route has opened and the eardrum can return toward its normal position.
