Paper cuts often happen on fingertips, lips, or between fingers, places packed with sensory nerve endings. Your hands need fine touch to handle objects, so the brain receives detailed signals from these areas. Even a small injury can therefore produce a very clear alarm.

The cut is usually shallow. It may slice through the outer skin and expose irritated nerve endings without reaching larger blood vessels. With little bleeding, a thick clot or scab may not cover the opening immediately. Air, water, soap, and pressure can keep stimulating it.

Paper also makes a less tidy edge than a surgical blade when viewed up close. Its fibers can create a rough microscopic injury. Then ordinary hand movements repeatedly pull the edges apart. A finger bends, taps, grips, and rubs against surfaces all day.

Pain does not measure an injury's size like a ruler. It reflects which nerves were disturbed, how exposed they remain, and how often the area is moved. That combination lets a cut almost too small to see demand a surprising amount of attention.
