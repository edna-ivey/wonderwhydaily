Humans cannot breathe on Mars because its atmosphere is extremely thin and made mostly of carbon dioxide, with only a tiny trace of oxygen.

At the Martian surface, air pressure is less than one percent of Earth's average sea-level pressure. Even if the gas mixture contained more oxygen, such low pressure would not deliver enough oxygen to the bloodstream. Unprotected body fluids and lungs would also face dangerous pressure conditions.

The atmosphere is about 95 percent carbon dioxide. Oxygen makes up only a small fraction, roughly one-tenthousandth of the density we have near Earth's surface. Mars is also cold, exposed to more radiation, and often dusty, so breathable gas is only one part of the protection explorers need.

Future astronauts would live in pressurized habitats and wear spacesuits outside. They could bring oxygen, split water molecules, or manufacture it locally. NASA's toaster-sized MOXIE experiment aboard the Perseverance rover successfully separated oxygen atoms from carbon dioxide molecules in Martian air.

Mars is not an airless world, but the difference between some atmosphere and a human-supporting atmosphere is enormous. A visitor would need an engineered pocket of Earth around every breath.
