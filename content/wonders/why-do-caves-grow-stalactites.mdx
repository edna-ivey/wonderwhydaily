Rainwater picks up carbon dioxide from air and soil, forming a weak acid. As it seeps through limestone, it dissolves a small amount of calcium carbonate. The water carries those dissolved ingredients through cracks toward a cave.

When a drop reaches open cave air, some carbon dioxide escapes. That chemical change makes the water less able to hold all its calcium carbonate. A tiny calcite crystal is left on the ceiling. Repeated drops add ring after ring around the same point.

A thin hollow tube called a soda straw may form first. If its central passage becomes blocked, water flows around the outside, depositing calcite over a wider surface and creating the familiar tapered stalactite. Drops landing below can build a stalagmite upward.

Growth rates vary enormously with water supply, chemistry, and climate. Oils from a hand can interfere with deposition, and broken formations cannot be quickly replaced. Cave decorations that look frozen in time are still growing, but usually on a geologic schedule.
