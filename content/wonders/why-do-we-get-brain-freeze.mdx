One second you are eating something frozen. The next, a sharp ache appears behind your forehead, even though nothing cold ever touched that part of your head.

Brain freeze starts when something very cold hits the roof of your mouth or the back of your throat. That sudden chill activates nerves in the area, especially pathways connected with the trigeminal nerve, a major nerve that carries feeling from the face and head.

Your brain receives the warning quickly, but it can misread exactly where the signal came from. The pain began near your mouth, yet it feels as if it belongs in your forehead or temples. That kind of misplaced feeling is called referred pain.

Blood vessels may also react to the sudden cold by narrowing and then widening again as the area warms. Scientists are still studying the full chain of events, but the result is familiar: a short, intense headache that fades once your mouth warms back up.

Brain freeze is not your brain freezing. It is a fast alarm from cold-sensitive tissues, routed through nerves that can make your forehead take the blame.
