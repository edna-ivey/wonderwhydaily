Comets release dust and small rocky particles as sunlight heats their icy surfaces. Instead of remaining in one clump, the debris spreads along the comet's path around the Sun. Some asteroids also create streams associated with meteor showers.

Earth follows nearly the same orbit each year. When that orbit intersects a debris stream, our planet reaches the crossing at roughly the same calendar time. The particles slam into the upper atmosphere at many kilometers per second, heating and ionizing the air around them to make bright streaks.

A shower may grow stronger or weaker from year to year. Gravity shifts debris, fresh comet passages add material, and moonlight or clouds change what observers can see. The named shower's radiant is the point from which its meteors appear to spread because of perspective.

The streak is not a star falling. It is a brief atmospheric glow caused by a tiny visitor arriving at enormous speed. The annual return is simply orbital geometry repeating itself.
