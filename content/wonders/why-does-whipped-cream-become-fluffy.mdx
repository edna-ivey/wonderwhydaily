Cream contains water, milk proteins, and many tiny fat droplets. When a whisk moves through it, the wires drag air into the liquid and divide that air into bubbles. At first the bubbles escape or merge easily, so the cream remains loose.

Cold cream contains some solid fat crystals. Whipping damages the protective layers around fat droplets, allowing them to partly stick together without merging into one oily mass. These clustered droplets gather around air bubbles and connect into a network that supports the foam.

As more air is incorporated, the cream expands and becomes stiff enough to hold peaks. Temperature matters because warm fat is too liquid to build the same stable framework. A chilled bowl and whisk help the cream stay in the useful partly solid state.

Whipping has a limit. Continued beating pushes more fat droplets together, squeezing out liquid and collapsing the foam. The mixture separates into butter and buttermilk. Fluffy cream and butter are therefore different stopping points along the same physical process.
