A beach can seem to breathe. In many places, water creeps up the sand, slips away, and returns hours later because the Moon and Earth's rotation create a repeating tidal pattern.

The Moon's gravity pulls on the whole planet, including the oceans. That pull is slightly stronger on the side of Earth closer to the Moon and slightly weaker on the far side. Those differences help create tidal bulges.

As Earth rotates, many coasts pass through that tidal pattern. That is why lots of places get two high tides and two low tides during a lunar day, which is a little longer than a normal 24-hour day.

Real coastlines make the story messier. Continents block the water. Ocean basins have different shapes and depths. Bays, seafloors, and the turning Earth can redirect the moving water. Some places get one main high tide per day, and others get two tides of very different heights.

The Sun also matters. When the Sun, Moon, and Earth line up, their combined pull can create a larger difference between high and low tide. When the Sun and Moon pull from different directions, that difference is usually smaller.

So tides are not just water following the Moon overhead. The Moon starts a planet-sized rhythm, and every coastline plays its own version.
