The Moon looks bigger near the horizon because your brain judges it differently when it appears beside horizon scenery.

The Moon is still about the same actual size in the sky. If you photograph it near the horizon and later high overhead using the same camera zoom, the Moon's image will be nearly the same width. That is a good clue that the change is happening in perception.

Near the horizon, the Moon appears beside trees, buildings, hills, or distant landscape. Your brain uses those cues while interpreting size and distance. High overhead, the Moon sits in a mostly empty sky with fewer familiar objects around it. The same disk can therefore feel very different.

Scientists still study exactly why the Moon illusion feels so strong. Several perception clues may work together, so this is not a perfectly solved magic trick. But the important part is that Earth's atmosphere is not magnifying the Moon into a giant disk, and the Moon is not suddenly much closer when it rises.

The Moon illusion is a wonderful reminder that seeing is not simply measuring. Your brain is constantly building a story about size and distance, and sometimes that story makes the sky feel gloriously larger than it is.
