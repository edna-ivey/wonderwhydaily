Rivers carry broken rock, soil, and organic material downstream. Fast, turbulent water can keep many of those particles suspended. When a river reaches a lake or ocean, it spreads out and slows down, losing some of the energy that held the sediment aloft.

The heaviest grains settle first, while finer silt and clay travel farther. Layer after layer can accumulate around the river mouth. If sediment arrives faster than waves, tides, and currents remove it, the deposit grows outward and creates a delta.

As the delta builds, the river may split into smaller channels called distributaries. Sediment can clog one route and steer water into another, so a delta is constantly changing. Floods deliver especially large pulses of fresh material.

Not every river forms a large delta. Strong waves or deep coastal water may carry sediment away before it can build land. Where deltas do grow, they create rich wetlands, but dams, sinking ground, and rising seas can upset the balance that keeps them above water.
