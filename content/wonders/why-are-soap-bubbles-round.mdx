A soap bubble is a thin layer of water sandwiched between soap molecules, with air on both sides. Molecules in the liquid attract one another, creating surface tension. The film behaves a little like a stretchy skin that is always pulling itself tighter.

For any fixed amount of trapped air, a sphere has the smallest possible surface area. Because using less area lowers the film's energy, a free bubble naturally settles into a sphere. It does not matter whether the wand that launched it was a circle, square, or star once the film is floating freely.

A bubble is not perfectly rigid. Air pressure inside pushes outward while surface tension pulls inward, and those forces balance. Air currents, gravity, evaporation, and nearby surfaces can wobble or distort it. Small bubbles look rounder because surface tension matters more compared with their weight.

Bubbles behave differently when packed into foam. Neighbors press against one another and flatten shared walls, making polygon-like cells. Even then, surface tension keeps arranging the films to use energy efficiently, turning a sink full of suds into a living lesson in geometry.
