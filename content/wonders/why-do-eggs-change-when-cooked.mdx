A raw egg flows because its proteins are folded up and floating in water. Heat changes the shape of those proteins.

Proteins are long molecules folded into particular forms. When an egg gets hot, those folded proteins start to unfold. Once they open up, they bump into neighboring proteins and form new links.

As more proteins connect, they create a loose three-dimensional network that traps water. The egg can no longer flow freely, so it changes from a slippery liquid into a soft solid.

This process is called coagulation. It is the reason scrambled eggs thicken, custards set, and egg whites turn firm. The clear white also turns opaque because the linked protein network scatters light.

More heat keeps tightening the network. Cook gently and the egg can stay tender. Cook too long or too hot and the proteins squeeze out water, making the egg rubbery or dry.

Cooking an egg is not just heating food. It is unfolding invisible chains until they catch one another.
