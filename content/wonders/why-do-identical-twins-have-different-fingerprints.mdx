Identical twins have different fingerprints because genes influence ridge development, but the final microscopic pattern is also shaped by each finger's unique conditions in the womb.

Identical twins begin with nearly the same DNA, so their fingerprints may share broad features such as loops, whorls, or arches. Yet genes do not specify the exact location of every ridge ending, fork, and island. Those fine details develop as layers of fetal skin grow and fold.

Small differences matter during that process. Each fingertip experiences its own pressure, position, growth rate, blood supply, and contact with surrounding tissue and amniotic fluid. Even fingers on the same hand do not occupy identical environments. Tiny variations get amplified into a ridge map that belongs to one finger alone.

The patterns form before birth and generally persist as a person grows because new skin follows the underlying ridge structure. Cuts that reach deeply enough can leave scars, but ordinary surface wear grows back in the same arrangement.

Identical DNA can build extremely similar bodies without producing carbon copies. Fingerprints are a visible record of the conversation between a genetic plan and thousands of unrepeatable moments during development.
