Auroras begin with activity from the Sun. Energy and charged particles disturb Earth's magnetic environment, and electrons are guided along magnetic field lines toward polar regions. High above the ground, those electrons collide with atoms and molecules in the atmosphere.

A collision can lift an atom or molecule into an excited energy state. When it settles back down, it releases a photon of light. The color depends on the gas, its energy state, and the altitude where the collision occurs. Atomic oxygen commonly produces pale green light and can also produce red. Molecular nitrogen contributes blue and purple shades.

Height matters because the atmosphere grows thinner upward. Some excited states last long enough to emit light only where collisions are rare, while others shine lower down. This is one reason an auroral curtain can have green across its middle, red high above, and a purplish edge below.

The moving shapes trace changing flows of charged particles in Earth's magnetic field. An aurora is therefore both a light show and a visible clue that weather from the Sun is interacting with our planet.
