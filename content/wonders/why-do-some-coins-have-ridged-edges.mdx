When coins were made from gold or silver, their metal could be worth close to the value stamped on them. A dishonest person might shave a thin sliver from many coins, keep the precious metal, and spend the lighter coins at full value. This practice was called clipping.

Machine-made edge patterns made tampering visible. If a coin had evenly spaced reeds or lettering all the way around, removing metal disturbed the pattern. Merchants could inspect the edge instead of weighing every coin. More precise machinery also produced coins with consistent diameters and weights.

Modern circulating coins are usually worth more as money than as raw metal, so clipping is no longer the same threat. Ridged and smooth edges remain useful because they distinguish denominations by sight and touch. In the United States, a dime and a penny are similar in size, but their edges feel different.

Not every denomination is ridged. A mixture of edge designs makes the system more informative. A feature created to protect precious metal became a lasting piece of accessible, practical design.
