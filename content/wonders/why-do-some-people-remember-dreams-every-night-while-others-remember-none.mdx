Dream recall differs because having a dream, storing some of it as a memory, and retrieving that memory after waking are separate events that vary from person to person and night to night.

Dreams often happen during rapid eye movement sleep, though dreamlike experiences can occur in other stages. The sleeping brain is not recording events in exactly the same way it does while awake. A vivid experience can therefore leave a fragile memory trace that disappears within moments.

Timing matters. Waking during or soon after a dream gives the brain a chance to hold onto it. People who wake briefly more often may report more dreams, even if they do not spend more total time dreaming. Attention matters too: thinking about a dream immediately and writing it down strengthens retrieval.

Sleep patterns, stress, medications, age, and individual brain differences can all affect recall. Research in mice also suggests that some activity during REM sleep may help prevent unimportant experiences from being stored, though scientists cannot simply translate every mouse finding into a complete explanation of human dreams.

Saying 'I never dream' usually means 'I do not remember dreaming.' The nighttime story may have played; the morning brain just did not save a durable copy.
