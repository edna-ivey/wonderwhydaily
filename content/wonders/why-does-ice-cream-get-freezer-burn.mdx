Ice cream gets freezer burn when water molecules migrate out of exposed parts of the dessert and form larger ice crystals, leaving dry, rough, or icy patches.

A freezer is cold, but water can still move. Ice molecules at a food's surface can pass directly into water vapor, a process called sublimation. If air reaches the ice cream, that vapor may travel to a colder surface and freeze again. The original spot loses moisture while frost or large crystals grow elsewhere.

Temperature swings speed the damage. Each time ice cream warms slightly, some small crystals melt. When it cools again, the water tends to join existing crystals, making them larger. Those big crystals feel coarse on the tongue instead of creamy.

A tight lid, little empty air, and a steady cold temperature slow the process. Pressing food-safe wrap or parchment against the surface can reduce air contact. Storing ice cream toward the back of the freezer protects it from warm air entering whenever the door opens.

Freezer burn does not scorch food and generally does not make properly frozen food unsafe. It changes quality. Ice cream's smooth microscopic structure has simply been rearranged into something drier and crunchier.
