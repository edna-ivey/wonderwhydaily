A bruise forms when a bump damages tiny blood vessels without breaking the skin. Blood leaks into nearby tissue, where it cannot simply flow away through the usual circulation. The first mark may look red, purple, blue, or dark brown depending on skin tone, depth, and the amount of blood involved.

Then the body's cleanup systems get to work. Cells dismantle hemoglobin, the oxygen-carrying pigment in red blood cells. As hemoglobin and related molecules are converted into other compounds, the light reflected through the skin changes. Greenish and yellowish shades can appear before the pigments are carried away or reused.

The familiar sequence is not an exact clock. Bruises vary with location, injury depth, circulation, age, medicines, and skin color. Different parts of the same bruise can also be at different stages, creating several colors at once.

Most ordinary bruises fade as the leaked material is removed and the vessels repair themselves. A bruise that is unusually painful, appears often without a known cause, or comes with other concerning symptoms is something to discuss with a healthcare professional.
