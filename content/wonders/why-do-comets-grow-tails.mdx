Comets grow tails when sunlight warms their frozen nuclei, releasing gas and dust that radiation and the solar wind push away from the Sun.

Far from the Sun, a comet is a dark mixture of ice, rock, dust, and frozen gases. As it travels inward, heat turns some ice directly into gas. Jets carry dust off the surface, surrounding the nucleus with a glowing cloud called a coma.

Solar ultraviolet light can strip electrons from gas molecules in the coma, creating charged ions. The solar wind, a flow of charged particles from the Sun, sweeps those ions into a narrow bluish tail. Sunlight pressure pushes dust more gently, creating a wider tail that curves along the comet's orbit.

Both tails point generally away from the Sun, not necessarily behind the comet. After the comet swings around the Sun and heads outward, its tails can appear in front of its direction of travel. They shrink again as the nucleus cools.

The dramatic tail is not the comet wearing away through friction with space. It is frozen material waking in sunlight and being sorted into streams by two different kinds of solar push.
