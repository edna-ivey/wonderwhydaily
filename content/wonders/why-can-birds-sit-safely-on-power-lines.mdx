Electric current flows when charge has a path between points at different electrical potentials, often described as different voltages. A bird perched with both feet on the same wire is brought to that wire's voltage, but the short stretch between its feet has almost no voltage difference.

With no useful push from one foot to the other, only a tiny amount of current takes the higher-resistance route through the bird. Most current continues through the metal wire. The bird's safety does not come from magical feathers or from switching electricity off.

The situation becomes dangerous if the bird touches two places with a large voltage difference. A wing contacting another wire, or a foot touching a grounded pole while the bird remains on the live wire, can create a path through its body. Power systems use spacing and insulators partly to prevent such connections.

People standing on the ground are in a different situation because touching a live conductor can connect that conductor toward ground through the body. Power lines should always be treated as dangerous, whether or not birds appear comfortable on them.
