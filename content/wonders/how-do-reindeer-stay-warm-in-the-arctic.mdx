Reindeer, also called caribou in North America, need to keep their core warm while living in cold, windy landscapes. Their coat has two important layers: soft, dense underfur near the skin and longer guard hairs outside. Many guard hairs are hollow, and both layers trap pockets of air.

Still air slows heat transfer, which is the same principle used in puffy jackets and double-pane windows. The outer coat also sheds snow and helps block wind. Fat stores provide energy and additional insulation, while a compact body reduces exposed surface area compared with a long, thin shape.

Their faces help too. Complex nasal passages warm and moisten cold incoming air. On the way out, those tissues can recover some heat and moisture from the breath instead of losing all of it to the Arctic air.

Insulation creates another challenge during activity: overheating. Reindeer can adjust blood flow and release heat from less-furry areas. Arctic survival is not about making endless warmth. It is about carefully controlling where heat is kept and where it can escape.
