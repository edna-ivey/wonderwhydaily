Venus is hotter than Mercury because its thick atmosphere traps heat extremely well.

Mercury sits closest to the Sun, so it seems obvious that it should win the solar system's hottest-planet contest. But Mercury has almost no atmosphere. Sunlight heats its surface fiercely during the day, and then much of that heat can escape into space when darkness arrives.

Venus is wrapped in a dense atmosphere made mostly of carbon dioxide. Sunlight reaches the surface and warms it. The planet then tries to send that energy back upward as heat, but the atmosphere absorbs and redirects much of it. Heat becomes trapped in a runaway greenhouse effect.

Clouds cover Venus, but they do not make the surface pleasantly shady. Beneath them, the thick atmosphere spreads heat around the planet so effectively that nights remain fiercely hot too.

Venus reveals a surprising planetary rule: where a world lives matters, but what surrounds it can completely rewrite the result. A planet farther from the Sun can become hotter by being terrible at letting heat leave.
