Robots need sensors because useful movement depends on detecting what is happening and adjusting.

A robot's motors can move wheels, arms, or joints, but movement alone does not tell the robot what happened. Did the wheel slip? Is there a table leg ahead? Did the gripper actually pick up the object? Without new information, the robot can only keep following instructions and hope the world matches the plan.

Sensors translate pieces of the physical world into data the robot's computer can use. Cameras measure light. Distance sensors detect nearby objects. Touch sensors notice contact. Encoders track how far a wheel or joint has turned.

That information creates a feedback loop. A robot moves, checks the result, and adjusts. A warehouse robot can slow when someone steps into its path. A robotic arm can stop squeezing when it detects enough force. A small rolling robot can correct when one wheel travels farther than the other.

Sensors do not make a robot magically aware. Engineers still decide what measurements matter and write rules or learning systems for responding to them. A sensor can also be fooled by glare, dirt, darkness, or an object it was not designed to detect.

Useful robots need some way to compare what was supposed to happen with what actually happened. Sensors are how that comparison begins.
