We get pins and needles when pressure temporarily disrupts a peripheral nerve and its blood supply, causing unusual electrical signals as conditions change and normal communication returns.

Sitting on a foot or leaning on an arm can compress nerves between bone and other tissue. The squeezed nerve fibers become less able to carry their usual messages. That can first make the area feel numb or heavy, which is why people say a limb has fallen asleep.

When you shift position, pressure eases and circulation improves. Different sensory fibers recover at different rates and may fire irregularly for a short time. The brain receives a scrambled burst of touch, temperature, and pain-related signals, interpreting them as prickling, buzzing, or tiny stabs.

The medical term is paresthesia. A brief episode caused by posture usually fades within minutes. Persistent tingling, repeated episodes without clear pressure, weakness, severe pain, or symptoms on one side of the body can have other causes and should be discussed promptly with an adult and healthcare professional.

No actual pins are moving under the skin. The feeling is the nervous system's report from communication lines that were compressed and are coming fully back online.
