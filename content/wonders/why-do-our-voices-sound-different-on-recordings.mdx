Your recorded voice sounds different because you normally hear yourself through both the air and vibrations traveling through your head, while a recording captures mainly the air-conducted sound.

When you speak, pressure waves leave your mouth and travel through the air into your ears. That is mostly how everyone else hears you. At the same time, vibration from your vocal tract travels through bone and soft tissue in your skull to the inner ear.

Bone conduction changes the balance of frequencies reaching you. It often strengthens lower-frequency sensations, helping your live voice seem deeper or fuller from inside your own head. A microphone does not capture that private internal route. Playback therefore resembles what other listeners hear more than what you have heard all your life.

Microphone quality, distance, room echoes, speakers, and audio compression can add more differences. There is also a familiarity effect: your brain has built a strong expectation for your bone-and-air mixture, so the outside-only version can feel oddly unlike you even when it is recognizable.

The recording has not stolen your real voice. It has simply recorded one listening path while leaving out the vibrations that normally give you an inside track.
