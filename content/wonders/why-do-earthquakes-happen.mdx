An earthquake is not the ground randomly deciding to wobble. It is stored movement escaping.

Earth's tectonic plates are always moving slowly, but the rocks along a fault can get stuck because of friction. While they are stuck, stress builds. The rock bends and stores energy, a little like a stretched spring.

When the stress finally overcomes the friction holding the fault in place, the rocks slip suddenly. That release sends seismic waves through the ground. Those waves are what people feel as shaking.

The slip does not have to start directly under your feet. Seismic waves can travel far from the fault where the earthquake began. That is why a quake can be felt across a large region, sometimes in places where nothing visible has cracked open.

After the main slip, nearby rock may need to adjust to the new arrangement of stress. That is one reason aftershocks can follow. The first jolt changes the underground balance, and the crust keeps settling.

The ground feels solid because its movements are usually slow or hidden. An earthquake is the moment that hidden strain becomes impossible to ignore.
