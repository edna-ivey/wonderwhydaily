Bananas turn brown after peeling because damaged fruit cells let oxygen meet an enzyme called polyphenol oxidase and the plant compounds it can transform.

Inside an intact banana, cell structures keep many ingredients separated. Peeling, slicing, or bruising breaks those tiny compartments. Oxygen from the air enters, and polyphenol oxidase helps oxidize naturally occurring phenolic compounds. A chain of reactions then creates dark pigments called melanins.

The reaction is part of the fruit's response to damage, not a sign that the banana instantly became unsafe. Texture and flavor will continue changing as the fruit ripens and loses moisture, but a little surface browning is mostly a quality issue.

You can slow the color change. Lemon or other acidic juice lowers the pH around the fruit, making the enzyme less effective. Keeping cut banana cool and limiting its contact with air can help too. The same broad kind of enzymatic browning happens in apples, pears, avocados, and potatoes.

So the brown color was not hiding underneath the peel. It was manufactured after the peel came off and the fruit's separated chemistry suddenly met the air.
