Mars looks red because much of the surface is coated with iron-rich dust and minerals that contain iron oxides.

Iron oxide is related to rust on Earth. That does not mean Mars is a giant rusty nail, but it is a useful comparison: iron reacts and ends up in compounds that can look reddish, orange, or brown.

The reddish dust on Mars is extremely fine, and winds can lift it into the thin atmosphere. From far away, that dust helps blend the planet into the red-orange world we recognize in the night sky.

Mars is not the same shade everywhere. Spacecraft images show darker volcanic rock, pale dust, tan ground, and many rusty tones. The famous red appearance is a planet-wide impression, not one flat paint color.

Scientists are still studying the exact history of how Mars's iron minerals formed and changed, including what role water may have played long ago. The color is familiar, but it points to a deep planetary history written in dust.
