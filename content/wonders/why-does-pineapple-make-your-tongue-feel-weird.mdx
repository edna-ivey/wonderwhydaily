Pineapple makes your tongue feel weird because the fruit contains several things that can irritate the delicate surface of your mouth.

Fresh pineapple contains bromelain, a group of enzymes that break proteins into smaller pieces. When you eat fresh pineapple, bromelain may briefly affect proteins in the protective surface of your tongue, cheeks, and lips. Your saliva and digestive system quickly handle the enzyme, and the cells lining your mouth repair themselves fast, so the sensation usually does not last.

Pineapple also contains calcium oxalate crystals called raphides. They are tiny, needle-like structures found in some plants. Chewing the fruit can release them, and that mechanical scratchiness may add to the prickly feeling. Then pineapple's acidity can make a sensitive mouth notice the irritation even more, especially after a large serving.

The odd feeling is usually strongest with fresh pineapple because cooking changes bromelain's shape and quiets that part of the chemistry. Canned or cooked pineapple often feels gentler, even though the fruit can still be acidic.

So the fruit is not attacking you, exactly. For a few minutes, your tongue is meeting enzymes, acid, and microscopic plant crystals all at once.
