A smooth shiny surface reflects light in an orderly way: the outgoing angle matches the incoming angle. Tinsel is made of many narrow reflective strips that bend, twist, overlap, and sway. Each little surface points in a somewhat different direction.

At any moment, only some pieces are angled to send a lamp's light into your eyes. As you, the decoration, or the light moves, those alignments change. One highlight vanishes and another appears. Your visual system interprets the rapid scattered flashes as sparkle.

Narrow strips also have many edges and curves, breaking reflections into small bright points instead of one large image. Darker gaps between pieces increase the contrast and make highlights seem especially brilliant.

Modern tinsel is commonly metallized plastic, but older versions used metal, including silver. The materials changed; the optical trick remained. Sparkle comes from multiplying reflective angles and setting them in motion.

The effect becomes stronger near small bright bulbs because each compact light source can create a sharp, easily noticed highlight.
