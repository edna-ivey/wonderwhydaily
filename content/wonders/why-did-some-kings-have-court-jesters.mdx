Some kings kept court jesters because royal households needed entertainment, and a talented fool could also use humor to release tension or express ideas that formal courtiers avoided.

Jesters were not one standard job with one uniform and rulebook. Depending on the place and century, a court fool might tell stories, sing, juggle, perform physical comedy, imitate people, trade clever remarks, or join ceremonies. Some were wandering entertainers before gaining a court position; others lived under a ruler's patronage.

A palace surrounded a monarch with rank and ceremony. Open disagreement could be risky, but a joke could turn criticism into play. Famous jesters sometimes mocked a ruler, exposed vanity, or commented on political behavior with unusual freedom. That freedom was never absolute, and colorful stories told later can exaggerate how much any jester could safely say.

Their lives also complicate the cheerful costume image. Historical courts sometimes treated people with physical or cognitive differences as curiosities. Status ranged from valued performer and companion to dependent servant, and experiences differed enormously.

The jester's power came from occupying two positions at once: inside the court but outside its normal dignity. Laughter entertained the room, while occasionally giving an uncomfortable truth permission to enter.
