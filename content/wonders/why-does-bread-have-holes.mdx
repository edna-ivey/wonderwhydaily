Bread has holes because gas bubbles get trapped, expand, and then set inside the dough.

The related Wonder about why bread rises explains the first big move: yeast feeds on sugars and releases carbon dioxide gas. This Wonder is about what happens to those bubbles afterward.

Bread dough made with wheat flour can form gluten, a stretchy network of proteins. Gluten lets the dough hold gas instead of letting every bubble escape immediately. As fermentation continues, bubbles grow, press against one another, and stretch the dough around them.

During baking, gases expand and water turns to steam, pushing the bubbles larger for a while. Then heat sets the starches and proteins. The dough firms into bread, and the bubble spaces remain as holes after the gas escapes and the loaf cools.

The pattern of holes is called the crumb. A soft sandwich loaf usually has a tighter crumb with many small, even holes. A wet dough handled gently and fermented longer can develop larger, irregular openings. Bakers can steer the crumb by changing hydration, mixing, folding, proofing time, and shaping.

So the holes are not missing bread. They are the architecture left behind by bubbles that the dough managed to hold.
