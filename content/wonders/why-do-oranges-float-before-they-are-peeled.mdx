An object floats when its average density is lower than the liquid around it. Orange flesh contains lots of water and dissolved sugars, so the juicy peeled fruit can be slightly denser than plain water.

The rind looks solid, but its white pith and outer peel contain many tiny air spaces. Air adds volume with very little mass. When the peel surrounds the fruit, the orange's total mass is spread over a larger volume, lowering its average density below that of water.

Peeling removes some mass, but it removes a disproportionately buoyant part. The remaining fruit has less volume and loses the air-pocket layer, so its average density can rise. It may sink even though it weighs less than before.

This is the same reason a heavy steel ship can float while a compact steel block sinks. Shape and enclosed air determine average density. Floating depends not on weight alone, but on weight compared with displaced water.
