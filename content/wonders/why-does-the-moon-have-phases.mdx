The Sun always illuminates half of the Moon, just as it illuminates half of Earth. The Moon does not make its own visible light. Its bright portion reflects sunlight.

As the Moon travels around Earth, the angle among Sun, Moon, and observer changes. Near new moon, the lit half faces mostly away from Earth. A growing crescent appears, followed by first quarter and gibbous phases. At full moon, the illuminated half faces us most directly.

After full moon, the visible lit portion shrinks through the same sequence in reverse. The complete cycle takes about 29.5 days because Earth is also moving around the Sun while the Moon orbits us.

Earth's shadow creates a lunar eclipse, not the ordinary monthly phases. An eclipse requires a close alignment at full moon, and the Moon's tilted orbit usually carries it above or below the shadow.

Observers everywhere see the same phase at a given moment, although the bright shape can appear rotated depending on their latitude and viewing angle.
