Many squirrels prepare for lean times by caching food. Instead of placing every nut in one pantry, a gray squirrel often uses scatter hoarding, burying individual nuts across many locations. Spreading the supply means one thief or one flooded patch is less likely to erase the whole winter menu.

Finding those nuts again involves memory and clues from the landscape. Researchers have found that squirrels organize caches and can remember many locations, although smell may also help at close range. They sometimes dig up and rebury food, and a squirrel that suspects it is being watched may pretend to hide a nut before carrying it elsewhere.

The strategy is not flawless. Other animals steal some caches, and squirrels fail to recover others. Those forgotten acorns are not necessarily wasted. If conditions are right, an uneaten seed can sprout far from its parent tree, helping forests renew and spread.

So the frantic autumn digging has two outcomes. It stocks a squirrel's future meals, and by accident it can plant part of a future forest.
